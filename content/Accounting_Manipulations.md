# Accounting Manipulations (Earnings Management)

## Summary
Accounting manipulations are techniques used by management to make reported earnings appear higher, smoother, or more predictable than the underlying economic reality. [[Nick_Sleep]] returns to this theme repeatedly in the Nomad letters as both a red flag when screening companies and an opportunity when the market over-penalises a business for manipulation that left free cash flow untouched. The key antidote is always the same: anchor on cash flow, not earnings.

## Explanation

### The Core Tension
Reported earnings are an accounting construct subject to enormous management discretion. Cash flow — the actual movement of money — is far harder to fake and is Sleep's primary valuation anchor. When a divergence opens between earnings and cash flow, something is usually wrong on one side or the other.

### Case Study: Xerox and SFAS 13

The most detailed manipulation analysis in the early Nomad letters concerns [[Xerox_Corporation]] and the abuse of **SFAS 13 (Statement of Financial Accounting Standards No. 13 — Accounting for Leases)**.

**The mechanism:**
Under SFAS 13, a lease is classified as either:
- **Operating lease**: Short-term rental; payments booked incrementally over the lease term.
- **Sale-type lease**: Long-term; transfers 75%+ of the asset's economic life or where minimum payments ≥ 90% of fair value. Treated as if the asset was sold outright — revenues and profits recognised immediately.

Xerox sold expensive printers (~US&#36;100,000+) in multi-year bundles that included equipment, service, financing, and maintenance. The bundle gave management discretion in allocating value between equipment (recognised immediately if sale-type) and service/financing (deferred). By inflating the equipment portion, contracts could be reclassified from operating to sale-type leases, accelerating revenue and profit recognition.

**The result:**
- US&#36;6.4bn of revenues incorrectly booked over five years out of ~US&#36;90bn total (~7%).
- Cumulative profits were pulled forward, flattering reported earnings per share.
- Cash flow was **completely unchanged** throughout — customers paid their monthly bills as agreed regardless of how Xerox classified the contract.

**The exposure:**
When earnings fell short of the 15% p.a. EPS target, the SEC launched an investigation, auditors were replaced, and the commercial paper market essentially closed to Xerox. The share price fell to **7% of its peak**. The irony: once the restatement was done, the new (correct) accounting methodology would produce *higher* reported revenues and profits going forward than the discredited method.

### Why Management Manipulate
Sleep does not treat manipulation as a random pathology — he traces it directly to incentive structure. At Xerox, management had stock options and bonuses tied to EPS growth. Wall Street rewarded EPS growth above all else. The two forces together created enormous pressure to find growth from any source, legitimate or not.

> "Management asserted that earnings per share would rise by 15% per annum...management, plump with stock options and growth in earnings per share related bonuses, were strongly incented to recognise earnings up front." — Letter 3, December 2002

This is a classic agency problem: short-term financial incentives misaligned with long-term business economics. See [[Owner_Oriented_Management]] for the positive case.

### The Investor Implication
When manipulations are discovered, the market tends to over-penalise:
1. The restatement is often the end of uncertainty, not the beginning.
2. If cash flow was not affected, the *economic* value of the business has not changed — only the accounting story.
3. The stock decline creates a deep value entry point for investors who can distinguish accounting noise from economic reality.

Sleep's framing:
> "It is important to put this in perspective. In the five years to 2001, cumulative revenues at Xerox approached US&#36;90bn, of which US&#36;6.4bn was incorrectly booked. Cash flow and free cash flow, the basis of our valuation of the firm, is completely unaffected." — Letter 3, December 2002

### Other Manipulation Patterns Noted in the Letters
- **Earnings management via growth targets**: Setting unrealistic targets forces management into creative accounting to meet them (Xerox).
- **Hiding contingent liabilities in footnotes**: Monsanto's Solutia PCB indemnification buried in notes to accounts — see [[Monsanto_Company]].
- **"Window dressing"**: Mentioned in Letter 3 as a practice Sleep explicitly rejects for Nomad's own portfolio reporting.

### The Antidote
Sleep's consistent prescription: **read the footnotes, anchor on cash flow, be suspicious of the gap between reported earnings and operating cash generation.** He describes the research process as detective work — the manipulation is almost always visible if you look for it.
