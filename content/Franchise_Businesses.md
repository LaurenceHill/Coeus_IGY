# Franchise Businesses ("Difficult to Copy")

## Summary
"Franchise businesses" is [[Nick_Sleep]]'s term for companies with durable, hard-to-replicate competitive advantages — strong brands, licences, exclusive relationships, or embedded customer habits that protect economics from competition. These form the most valued category in the [[Portfolio_Classification_Framework]] and are the primary source of Nomad's long-run compounding.

## Explanation

### Definition
Sleep uses "franchise" in the classic Buffett/Munger sense — not a legal franchise arrangement but a business that can sustain above-average economics over time because it is genuinely difficult for competitors to copy. The key word is **difficult**: not impossible, but hard enough that competitors are deterred or disadvantaged.

Characteristics Sleep associates with franchise businesses:
- Pricing power (customers cannot easily substitute)
- Recurring or captive revenues (membership fees, subscription, long-term contracts)
- Regulatory protection or exclusive licences
- High switching costs or brand loyalty built over decades
- Scale economics that reinforce the moat

### Why Franchises Are Nomad's "Current Winners"
In Letter 2 (June 2002), Sleep notes that franchise holdings were the source of outperformance in the early period, while deep value workouts were the underperformers. This is structurally expected: franchise businesses tend to hold value in declining markets because their economic moat insulates them from the cycle.

### The Franchise Paradox
Great franchise businesses rarely trade at the deep discounts Sleep requires. Letter 2 illustrates this directly with **Estée Lauder**:

> "Estée Lauder, a company which we much admire and would consider owning if only someone would sell it to us at a reasonable price…" — Letter 2, June 2002

Even after a 15% price drop on a short-term earnings warning, Estée Lauder was not cheap enough for Nomad. This highlights the core tension for value investors: the best franchises are usually well-understood and well-owned.

### Relationship to [[Margin_of_Safety]]
Franchise businesses tend to command higher prices, so the margin of safety on entry is typically smaller. Sleep compensates by seeking franchises that are (a) temporarily misunderstood, (b) in recovering cycles, or (c) in markets (Asia, South Africa) where sophisticated buyers are fewer.

### The "Detective Work" Framing
Sleep quotes Chris Browne of Tweedy Browne: the research process is like detective work or investigative journalism. This applies especially to franchises, where the "moat" is qualitative and not visible in near-term financials.
