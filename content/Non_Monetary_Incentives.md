# Non-Monetary Incentives

## Summary
[[Nick_Sleep]]'s framework from Letter 24 (June 2013), drawing on the work of behavioural economist Dan Ariely, for understanding why the best founders, managers, and investors are not primarily motivated by money — and why this is *good news* for long-run performance. The core insight: meaning, identity, challenge, and creativity can be more powerful motivators than financial reward, and organisations (or funds) that tap these non-monetary incentives produce better outcomes than those that rely on pay alone. This connects directly to [[Character_as_Moat]], [[Principal_Agent_Conflict]], and Sleep's own approach to Nomad's fee structure.

## Explanation

### Why Do People Climb Mountains?
Sleep opens the incentive discussion in Letter 24 with a simple question: why do people climb mountains? There is no financial reward. The question is directed at anyone designing a compensation arrangement: before reaching for the incentive package, ask what motivates the person you are trying to motivate. Money is not the only answer — and for the best people, it may not even be the primary one.

> "Conventional wisdom has, in our view, quite correctly drawn a Pavlovian link between financial incentives and behaviour, money is not the only reason that people behave the way they do. Those tasked with setting compensation arrangements may first wish to ask themselves, why do people climb mountains?" — Letter 24, June 2013

### Dan Ariely: Three Experiments

#### 1. The Lego Robots (Sisyphus vs. Rewards)
Professor Dan Ariely (author and founder of "The Institute for Advanced Hindsight") described an experiment in which two groups were paid to build model robots from Lego-like blocks, with the fee lowered for each successive model until the builder stopped.

- **Rewards group**: Finished robots were placed carefully on a shelf — visible, preserved.
- **Sisypheans**: Finished robots were broken up immediately in front of the builder and the parts returned to rebuild.

The Sisypheans built fewer robots and stopped at a much higher price (i.e. earlier). The **magnitude** of the difference surprised the researchers: the rewards group produced more than **50% more robots** for a much lower price. The researchers concluded that destroying the models removed the *meaning* the Sisypheans saw in their work — and that the loss of meaning was not compensated by pay.

#### 2. The Powdered Cake Mix
In the 1940s, food companies introduced powdered cake mixes requiring only water. Sales were terrible. A reformulated product requiring the cook to add fresh eggs, milk and sugar — taking longer — paradoxically *improved* sales. Sleep's interpretation: the perceived value of the end product is influenced by the input of the consumer's own effort. The builder of Ikea furniture and the baker of cakes both attach extra meaning to their own handiwork. **Effort creates attachment.**

#### 3. The Origami Experiment
Ariely tasked one group with building a complex origami model *with instructions* and another *without instructions*. Both groups were then asked how much they would pay to keep their model (i.e. buy it back from the researcher).

Findings:
- Both groups were prepared to pay significantly more than the market price for professionally built origami — even though their own models were inferior.
- The group that built *without instructions* (and produced the worst results) were prepared to pay the **most** — the normal relationship between price and quality was inverted by the value placed on one's own effort.

The implication for investing: founders and long-tenured managers who have built something from scratch attach a meaning and identity to their work that cannot be replicated by a hired hand. This is one reason [[Owner_Oriented_Management]] produces better outcomes than professional management.

### Adam Smith vs. Karl Marx
Ariely frames the debate historically: Adam Smith saw the gains from specialisation (subdividing pin manufacturing into twelve steps) as a triumph of efficiency. Karl Marx worried about the estrangement felt by workers who become anonymous cogs in a larger process. The debate between efficiency and meaning has continued ever since. Sleep's implicit resolution: the best businesses find a way to embed meaning in the work itself — so that neither efficiency nor motivation are sacrificed.

### Application to Nomad's Portfolio Companies
Sleep identifies three founders in Nomad's portfolio who have, in his assessment, moved beyond personal financial motivation:

- **Nick Robertson (Asos)**: His paper net worth "has increased hugely" since Sleep has known him. Asked if he has thoughts of leaving, "his face lights up with the future possibilities of his firm and says he is having more fun now than ever before." See [[Asos]].
- **Jim Sinegal (Costco, before retirement)**: See [[Costco_Wholesale]].
- **Lord Harris (Carpetright)**: See [[Aggregation_of_Marginal_Gains]].

Sleep also cites **Jeff Bezos (Amazon)** and **Warren Buffett (Berkshire Hathaway)** as founders who have "substantially and permanently waived their salaries, bonuses, or option packages." The inference: at the highest level of founder-run businesses, the financial incentive has become largely irrelevant to motivation.

> "The best entrepreneurs we know don't particularly care about the terms of their compensation packages... We would surmise that the founders of the firms Nomad has invested in are not particularly motivated by the incremental dollar of personal wealth." — Letter 24, June 2013

### Application to Sleep and Zakaria
Letter 24 applies the same framework to Sleep and Zakaria themselves. The performance fee hurdle of 6% per annum is, mathematically, never actually 6% — due to the asymmetry of linked gains and losses and the fact that the fee reserve is itself invested in Nomad shares (condemned to buy high and sell low). Sleep acknowledges the naivety of the original design but is "quite relaxed" about it: the hurdle was always philosophically motivated, not precisely calculated, and Sleep derives enough value from the meaning of the work — challenge, identity, creativity, sense of job well done — that the precise financial calibration matters less than the philosophy behind it.

> "By having an attitude that is somewhat independent of financial rewards, we are sure that Nomad's performance in the long run will be far better too." — Letter 24, June 2013

This connects directly to [[Principal_Agent_Conflict]]: incentives and rules can *de-moralise* behaviour. A fund manager who is obsessed with the financial rewards of managing money will make subtly different (and subtly worse) decisions than one who has transcended those rewards. The "sense of right and wrong" that Sleep invokes in the fee discussion is itself a non-monetary motivator.
