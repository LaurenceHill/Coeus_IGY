# Probability-Based Thinking ("The Way Zeckhauser Plays Bridge")

## Summary
Probability-based thinking — assessing outcomes as decision trees with explicitly attached probabilities, updated as facts change — is one of the four major competitive advantages [[Nick_Sleep]] identifies for investors, and the one he considers most under-practised. Introduced via [[Charlie_Munger]]'s reference to Richard Zeckhauser, the world bridge champion turned Harvard behavioural economist, it is the subject of Sleep's formal speech "So, How Does Zeckhauser Play Bridge?" delivered to the Investment Fund for Foundations (September 2005), reproduced in full as an appendix to Letter 9. It is also the unifying framework behind Sleep's four-part analysis of investor psychological misjudgement.

## Explanation

### Richard Zeckhauser and the Bridge Analogy
Sleep spent approximately a year tracking down Richard Zeckhauser after Munger cryptically cited him:

> "The right way to think is the way Zeckhauser plays bridge, it's just that simple." — Charlie Munger

Zeckhauser was world bridge champion in 1966. He now runs a behavioural finance course at Harvard's Kennedy School of Government — notably rejected by the Harvard Economics Department as "not economics," placing it at the Kennedy School instead. His break with mainstream economics: the rational utility-maximising framework is wrong. Human beings have systematic biases (wobbles) in their decisions.

**How he plays bridge**: Zeckhauser thinks via **decision trees** and attaches **probabilities to the branches**. As facts change, he changes the probabilities. This approach allows him to be indifferent to individual outcomes — he is neither risk-averse nor risk-seeking in the usual sense; he simply optimises for expected value across the tree.

Applied to investing: "when you are comfortable dealing with probabilities, and the vast expanse of opportunities such as the global stock and bond markets, you don't have to be too conservative with your bets."

### Why Most People Cannot Think Probabilistically
Sleep offers two famous demonstrations:

**1. The Monty Hall Problem** — Named after the host of 1970s game show "Let's Make a Deal": A contestant chooses one of three doors. One has a car; two have goats. The host (who knows what is behind each door) opens one of the other two doors to reveal a goat. Should the contestant switch to the remaining closed door? **Yes — switching wins 2/3 of the time.** When this was posed in the New York Times, 10,000 letters arrived insisting the wrong answer was right. The mind is not naturally arranged to answer probability-based questions.

**2. The Disease Test** — One person in a thousand has a disease. A test is 99% accurate. Your friend tests positive. What is the probability your friend has the disease? The answer is **approximately 1 in 11** (not 1 in 100, which is the test's accuracy rate). Most doctors get this wrong — which is why the General Medical Council requires testing for diseases like HIV twice.

### The Four Psychological Misjudgements — Letter 9
Sleep identifies four causes of investor misjudgement (elaborated at length in the speech appendix):

**1. Social Proof / Group Psychology**
Stanley Milgram's experiment: as the size of a crowd looking up at nothing increased, so did the proportion of passers-by who stopped and looked up. The relationship was non-linear — step-function thresholds. In markets this produces waves of overinvestment (Thai cement in the mid-1990s; US telecom in the late-1990s). Combine social proof with envy and financial incentives and the result is catastrophic.

The Matichon example: institutional shareholders of [[Matichon]] sold when approached by a Thai PM-backed bidder — not because the investment case had changed, but because they were following each other, because the stock had become illiquid, and because of business pressure. Sleep declined to sell and identified all four psychological mistakes the institutions were making simultaneously.

**2. Availability Heuristic**
The tendency to over-weight vivid or easily obtained evidence. Dick Thaler's gorilla suit video: when asked to count basketball passes by the white team, most people fail to notice a man in a gorilla suit walk across the screen, beat his chest, and leave. We concentrate so hard on the task that we miss the bigger picture.

In investing: the most important information is often **not** in the headlines. What accountants expense (advertising, R&D, marketing) is valued at zero by the market. What auditors ignore entirely (product integrity, management character, customer loyalty) is frequently more valuable than reported earnings. "If it's in the headlines, it's in the price."

Sleep's example (from the speech): Estée Lauder's share price fell when it announced a rise in advertising spend — the market treated "all costs as bad costs." But advertising spend was building a brand asset expensed but not capitalised. The correct question is: what have they bought with that advertising budget?

**3. Probability-Based Thinking Failures**
(See Zeckhauser above.) Most investment mistakes are failures of expected value calculation. The biggest mistake is selling a stock that goes on to rise ten-fold — but investors focus on avoiding losses (actual costs) rather than avoiding missed gains (opportunity costs).

Sleep's self-criticism: he "blew Argentina three years ago" by over-weighting vivid, temporary evidence — exactly the mistake the framework predicts.

**4. Patience**
The scarcest attribute in investing. Buffett's answer at the Berkshire AGM when asked "what is the main difference between yourself and the average investor?" — "patience." Jack Bogle: average stock holding period is down to 10 months; average mutual fund is held for 2 years. Quarterly reporting is "an obsessive-compulsive disorder."

Sleep's argument: this is not because investors are stupid — it is because two agents (fund manager and immediate client) try to demonstrate value to each other through activity, creating dysfunctional feedback loops. The solution is principal-to-principal relationships. See [[Principal_Agent_Conflict]].

### The Mauboussin Framework
From Michael Mauboussin (Legg Mason), cited in the speech:

> "Individuals who achieve the most satisfactory long-term results across various probabilistic fields (gambling, cards, horses, investing in common stocks) have more in common with one another than they do with participants in their own field."

The characteristics of successful probabilistic thinkers:
- Focus on **process vs. outcome** (good process with bad outcome > bad process with good outcome)
- Constant search for **favourable odds**
- Understanding of the **role of time** (patience)
- An **expected value mindset**: weigh probabilities and outcomes, not just outcomes
- **High awareness of the factors that distort judgement**

These correspond almost exactly to the four psychological misjudgements Sleep outlines.

### Behaviour Finance as the "New Economics in Waiting"
Sleep argues that the thinking of Zeckhauser and the Santa Fe Institute should be thought of as "the new economics in waiting" — it will only become mainstream once the old guard has died off. He and Zakaria joined the Santa Fe Institute — at the time, only the second European investment firm to do so. Sleep cites Brian Arthur's model: "the economy as a complex adaptive system that learns as agents learn."

> "Einstein's theory that space and time were relative had already been thought of by Lorenz. But what Einstein did was put it central to everything — whilst everyone else kept the concept peripheral. And it changed the world. That's how behavioural finance should be used. But it probably won't."

### Application to Emerging Markets
Sleep uses the probabilistic framework to explain Marathon/Nomad's superior performance in Asia:
- Asian markets dominated by two extreme types: short-term gambling Chinese retail investors and very long-horizon dynastic Chinese tycoons
- The institutional "fake specialisation" in Asian regional funds creates agents whose covert instruction is to buy growth, not value
- Sleep and Zakaria have no geographic mandate — they only go to Asia when the odds are favourable. Their competition is often the trade buyer who cannot negotiate 100% at market prices for minority positions.
