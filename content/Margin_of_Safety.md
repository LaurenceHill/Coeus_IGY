# Margin of Safety ("Dollar Bills for Fifty Cents")

## Summary
Margin of safety is the gap between a stock's intrinsic business value and the price paid. Sleep and Zakaria repeatedly express this as buying "dollar bills for fifty cents" — purchasing businesses at approximately half what they are worth. It is one of the three core investment criteria of the [[Nomad_Investment_Partnership]] and the primary source of both return and downside protection.

## Explanation

### The Core Idea
Intrinsic value is Sleep's estimate of the real, long-run worth of a business — approximated primarily through normalised free cash flow, capitalised R&D assets, or replacement cost, depending on the business type. Margin of safety is then the discount to that value at which Nomad buys.

Throughout the first two letters, Sleep consistently targets businesses priced at **approximately 50% of intrinsic value**:

> "In aggregate we estimate our investments are currently priced by the market at 51% of their real worth, that is to say in our opinion we have bought dollar bills for 51 cents." — Letter 2, June 2002

> "…these investments are priced in the market at around 50% of what we believe the businesses to be worth…" — Letter 3, December 2002

### Valuation Methods Used
Sleep is not dogmatic about method — he uses whatever is most appropriate to the business:

| Business Type | Valuation Approach Used |
|---|---|
| [[International_Speedway_Corporation]] | Replacement cost of circuits + DCF on contracted media rights |
| [[Matichon]] | Revenue multiple (0.75x), normalised FCF multiple (4x), comparison to Western peers |
| [[Xerox_Corporation]] | Free cash flow (FCF) yield, capitalised R&D value |
| [[Costco_Wholesale]] | Growth-adjusted cash cow valuation |
| [[Weetabix_Limited]] | DCF on modest FCF growth; private market transaction comparables (PepsiCo/Quaker Oats multiple) |

### Why the Discount Exists
Sleep identifies several recurring sources of mispricing:
1. **Short-termism** — investors punish companies that invest for the future at the expense of near-term earnings (e.g., Estée Lauder, Letter 2). See [[Short_Termism]].
2. **Temporary distress** — debt, accounting restatements, or management mistakes that obscure underlying value ([[Xerox_Corporation]], [[Stagecoach_Group]]).
3. **Cyclicality** — advertising-dependent businesses ([[Matichon]]) priced at trough-cycle earnings.
4. **Post-bubble sentiment** — broad indiscriminate selling creating pockets of value everywhere in 2001–2002.
5. **Structural illiquidity** — listing on Ofex or similar thin markets excludes institutional capital regardless of business quality ([[Weetabix_Limited]]).

### The "Real Worth" Concept
Sleep uses "real business value" or "real worth" as synonyms for intrinsic value. He is careful to distinguish it from accounting book value or reported earnings. Cash flow is paramount: "Cash flow is unchanged" is his refrain when accounting noise clouds reported numbers (most explicitly in the Xerox analysis in Letter 3).

### Margin of Safety and Holding Cash
When insufficient opportunities exist at the required discount, Nomad holds cash. In Letter 1, Nomad was ~28% cash at year end. Sleep frames cash not as a default but as the result of a disciplined refusal to compromise on price:

> "We are in no hurry to invest the money in companies that do not meet our criteria." — Letter 1, December 2001

By December 2002, cash was reduced to ~5% as Sleep deployed aggressively during peak market pessimism. By June 2003 (Letter 4), the portfolio had risen from buying at **50 cents on the dollar** to being valued at approximately **65 cents on the dollar** — and Sleep was actively recycling out of those appreciated positions back into new 50-cent dollars:

> "We are recycling shares that have performed well (in aggregate the portfolio is valued at around 65c on the dollar of intrinsic value, up from 50 cents in December) into new fifty-cent dollars." — Letter 4, June 2003

This is a critical discipline: the margin of safety requirement does not relax once a position has risen. Each position is evaluated continuously against the 50-cents threshold, and capital is redeployed when that threshold is no longer met.

By December 2003, despite Nomad's extraordinary +79.6% gain that year, the portfolio-level price-to-value ratio remained at **65 cents on the dollar** — unchanged from June 2003. Sleep had actively recycled appreciated positions (Stagecoach partially sold, Weetabix pending sale, Lucent Preferred sold above par) into new investments (New World Development, Telewest, Midland Realty, UnitedGlobal Communications). By June 2004 the ratio had moved to **61 cents on the dollar**, reflecting new investments at attractive prices drawn down from the subscription queue.

> "Nothing occupies your manager more than reducing the price to value ratio of the Partnership, as we believe this to be the best indicator of latent value and future performance." — Letter 5, December 2003

By December 2004, as markets broadly rose, the ratio had increased to approximately **73 cents on the dollar** ("the low seventy cents"). Sleep notes the market is "broadly and evenly priced" with few obvious pockets of undervaluation — a more challenging environment than 2001–2003. With the Partnership now closed, there are few incremental dollars to reinvest, limiting the damage from elevated reinvestment risk.

By June 2005, Sleep had improved the ratio back to approximately **68 cents on the dollar** ("upper sixty cents") by adding to still-attractive positions. He reframes the remaining gap in terms of opportunity rather than achievement:

> "The birds in the bush are around 47% (68c/100c) bigger than the bird in the hand. This compares with only around 37% (73c/100c) bigger at the end of last year." — Letter 8, June 2005

By December 2006 (Letter 11), the ratio had recovered: Sleep describes it as "in the upper 60 cents on the dollar, having risen from the low 60s in October" — reflecting both market movements and active portfolio repositioning into new ideas after the transition to [[Sleep_Zakaria_and_Company]].

### The Most Attractive Opportunity in Nomad's History — Letter 15 (December 2008)
Sleep writes in Letter 15 that the portfolio is priced at "meaningfully less than half our appraisal of the value of the businesses in the portfolio" — the widest discount in Nomad's history. The post-Lehman selloff had indiscriminately reduced prices across the SES portfolio, despite the underlying businesses (Amazon, Air Asia, Costco) continuing to gain market share.

> "Our estimate is that these businesses are now available at meaningfully less than half our appraisal of their value." — Letter 15, December 2008

This is the most extreme reading of the price-to-value ratio recorded in all the letters. Sleep explicitly states that the partnership would remain open for new subscriptions while this condition held — the mirror image of the 2004 closure (when insufficient cheap stocks were available). See [[Longevity_of_Compound]] and [[Slack]] for the strategic logic behind this posture.
