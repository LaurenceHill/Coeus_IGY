# Portfolio Concentration

## Summary
Portfolio concentration — holding relatively few positions in high-conviction ideas — is a core operating principle of [[Nomad_Investment_Partnership]] and the subject of extended argument in Letter 6 (June 2004). [[Nick_Sleep]] argues that industry-standard over-diversification is a marketing and career-protection device that destroys long-run client returns, dilutes governance oversight, and signals a near-total absence of genuine conviction.

## Explanation

### The Case for Concentration

The theoretical anchor is the [[Kelly_Criterion]]: if you have genuine analytical edge (say, 75% probability of being right on an idea), the mathematically optimal position size is ~47.5% of the portfolio. This is the framework Sleep uses to indict the industry.

Sleep's intuitive argument runs parallel:

> "In reality opportunities in which we are comfortable to deploy capital are rare, and the highest conviction ideas the rarest of them all. The issue then is how much to invest in each idea?" — Letter 6, June 2004

If ideas with 75%+ conviction are rare, the right response is to make them large — not to fill the portfolio with mediocre ideas at equal weight for the sake of "diversification."

### The Problem with Over-Diversification

Sleep identifies three distinct harms from holding too many stocks:

**1. Attention dilution — failure to police corporate behaviour**

> "The more stocks you own the less you care about each one individually. Attention paid to corporate governance, capital allocation, incentive compensation, accounting, and strategy has to be diluted as the number of stocks rises." — Letter 6, June 2004

A fund with 200 holdings cannot meaningfully monitor governance at each one. Management teams at these companies face no real scrutiny. Sleep argues this contributed to the corporate scandals of the early 2000s.

**2. Over-diversification as marketing, not analysis**

> "In our opinion, the massive over-diversification that is commonplace in the industry has more to do with marketing, making the clients feel comfortable, and the smoothing of results than it does with investment excellence." — Letter 6, June 2004

Clients find a 200-stock portfolio reassuring. The manager faces less career risk if no single position can derail performance. Both incentives push toward diversification regardless of the investment case.

**3. Logical inconsistency revealed by [[Kelly_Criterion|Kelly]]**

Working backwards: a 200-stock equal-weight portfolio implies ~0.5% per idea, which under Kelly implies conviction of barely above 50-50. That is no analytical edge.

### Nomad's Own Self-Critique
In a striking moment of intellectual honesty, Sleep applies this logic to Nomad itself:

> "The logical extension of this line of thought is that Nomad's portfolio concentration has at times been too low." — Letter 6, June 2004

By June 2004, the ten largest Nomad holdings were ~50% of assets, with a tail of ~20 smaller positions from the early days. Sleep describes this as "building a new portfolio within the old portfolio." The aspiration is clear: as capital is redeployed, concentration should rise toward the level the Kelly Criterion implies.

### The Governance Dividend of Concentration
High concentration is not just about return maximisation — it is also about **active ownership**. When a fund holds 10%+ of a company, it can influence outcomes (see [[Active_Ownership]]). At 0.2%, it cannot.

> "Would fund managers be so liberal with dysfunctional management if the holding was 20% of the portfolio rather than 0.2%? Of course not." — Letter 6, June 2004

Sleep cites the [[Weetabix_Limited]] takeover as a direct consequence of being too small a shareholder to block a transaction he considered value-destructive. The lesson: grow positions large enough to influence outcomes.

### Practical Constraints
The ideal is constrained by liquidity and market depth. The [[Union_Cement]] episode is instructive: Sleep wanted a 10% position in the Philippines' largest cement company, priced at 25% of asset replacement cost. Over six months of being almost the only buyer, he managed to acquire just 1% of fund assets (US&#36;1m). Holcim subsequently bought control at 5× his purchase price. The opportunity was clear; the position was tiny not by choice but by market reality.

### Nomad's Portfolio State (Letter 6, June 2004)
- Total holdings: just over 30
- Ten largest: ~50% of assets
- Geographic split: ~one-third SE Asia, ~one-fifth UK, ~one-fifth US, ~one-tenth Europe
- Asset class: ~70% common shares, ~10% corporate bonds (distressed), ~20% cash
- Price-to-value ratio: **61 cents on the dollar**

### Letter 7 Update (December 2004)
By December 2004, the concentration picture had improved materially:
- Ten largest holdings: **~65% of assets** (up from ~50% in June 2004)
- 40% of assets in South East Asia
- Overall: described as "concentrated" — "we have investments as small as 0.5% of assets" but the tail is being consciously run off

Sleep explicitly notes the friction: Nomad is "stapled to a fund management organisation three hundred times its size" (Marathon). When Nomad's orders are aggregated with Marathon's main funds, Nomad effectively behaves as a US&#36;30bn fund. "We look like a mouse but move like an elephant." This structural constraint limits the achievable concentration at any given liquidity level — independent of conviction.

### Letter 9 Update (December 2005)
By December 2005, concentration had increased further:
- **Ten stocks = ~70% of Partnership**
- The deliberate run-off of the early-period tail positions continued

The resolution of the Marathon aggregation constraint would follow in 2006 with the formation of [[Sleep_Zakaria_and_Company]], removing the structural barrier to acting as a small fund.

### Letters 11–12 Update (December 2006 – June 2007)
By Letter 11 (December 2006):
- **Five largest holdings = ~50% of the portfolio**
- **Ten largest holdings = ~75% of the portfolio**
- Remaining tail: ~20 positions including Zimbabwe baskets, distressed turnarounds, and "rats and mice from the early days"
- "Polarity" is now a deliberate feature: several large, simple, high-conviction holdings alongside a tail of more complicated but potentially higher-upside ideas

By Letter 12 (June 2007):
- **Ten stocks = ~80% of the Partnership** — concentration at its highest in the letters so far
- Over **85% of the Partnership in founder or first-generation managed businesses** (see [[Owner_Oriented_Management]])
- Amazon alone represents approximately **one sixth** (~16–17%) of the portfolio after a doubling in share price

### Inverted Portfolio Construction — Letter 12
Sleep introduces a new mental model for position sizing that reinforces the concentration argument:

> "It is commonplace for overall portfolio construction to be as a result of stock weightings built up from one to two to three percent of a portfolio and so on up to a target holding... There is another way to construct a portfolio, which is to invert and start at a hundred percent weighting and work down!" — Letter 12, June 2007

If fund managers started from 100% and asked "how much of this do I want to reduce my position?", they would end up with completely different (and far more concentrated) portfolios. The conventional anchoring at small initial positions is a structural impediment to acting on conviction.

Sleep acknowledges this self-criticism directly regarding Amazon: "in allowing past habits to anchor portfolio construction we have probably made the mistake of a starting holding that was almost certainly too low." See [[Kelly_Criterion]] for the parallel argument about optimal sizing and the first mistake of "not buying enough."

### Letter 16 Portfolio Breakdown (June 2009)
By Letter 16, Sleep categorises the portfolio explicitly by investment model:

| Model | Approx. % of portfolio |
|---|---|
| Scale Economies Shared (Amazon, Costco, Air Asia, etc.) | >50% |
| Replacement cost | ~15% |
| Hated agencies | ~15% |
| Super-quality thinkers | <10% |

Further structural data (Letter 16):
- **~20 investments** in total
- **Top 10 ≈ ~80% of NAV**
- **One investment ≈ ~30% of NAV** (Amazon, the result of the inverted construction logic applied over time)
- Revenue diversification: Letter 16 notes the portfolio generates revenues across multiple currencies and geographies, mitigating concentration at the revenue level even where position concentration is high

Sleep's explicit defence of ~30% in one stock references Sam Walton's quip: "That's easy — one of them is that I've only got one stock." Walton's entire fortune was in Wal-Mart.
