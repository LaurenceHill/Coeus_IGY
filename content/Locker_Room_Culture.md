# Locker Room Culture

## Summary
"Locker room culture" is [[Charlie_Munger]]'s term for the win-at-any-costs mentality that [[Nick_Sleep]] identifies as the dominant mindset of financial markets and corporate life. Introduced in Letter 17 (Annual December 2009), it describes an orientation that prioritises vivid short-term outputs — bonuses, earnings targets, market share rankings — over what is right for customers, companies, and society in the long run. Sleep uses it to explain both the financial crisis and the chronic underperformance of professional fund management.

## Explanation

### Munger's Diagnosis
Munger coined the phrase to describe the attitude of "players who just have to win, and are not too squeamish about the means." Sleep characterises it as a "proud, manipulative, points-scoring orientation that manifests itself in all walks of life, but may be especially evident on Wall Street." It is "inherently focused on vivid, short-term outputs."

The domain is broad: salesmen, sportsmen, chief executives, politicians, hedge fund managers. What unites them is the willingness to achieve short-term wins through means that are unsustainable or dishonest — with the consequences deferred to someone else's problem.

### The Mortgage Market as Extreme Case
The 2008 credit crisis is Sleep's primary exhibit. The mortgage-market version of the locker room mindset worked as follows:

1. Salesmen needed volume (bonus = short-term output).
2. They convinced investors to buy complex paper by packaging it with the language of "specialisation" and "diversification" — concepts that "sound wholesome and prudent enough, a little like motherhood and apple pie."
3. The products were neither genuinely diversified nor analysable. Their complexity made their risks invisible to buyers.
4. Result: a "US&#36;1 trillion case of 'careful what you wish for.'"

> "It is difficult to get a man to understand something when his salary (or bonus) depends on his not understanding it." — Upton Sinclair (cited Letter 17, December 2009)

### Schlock Begets Schlock
Sleep identifies a self-reinforcing feedback loop between the locker room culture in fund management and in the companies fund managers own:

> "We would argue that not only do companies get the investors they deserve, but investors also get the companies they deserve. Schlock begets schlock, as it were." — Letter 17, December 2009

If the shareholder base of a listed company is dominated by mutual funds seeking short-term performance, those fund managers will pressure management for short-term outputs. Management, in turn, will sacrifice the long term to hit the quarterly numbers. Both parties are "in denial" that this is happening — at least to their clients. The incentive alignment question: "how are the incentives aligned?" is almost never asked honestly.

### The Antithesis: Starting with the Customer
Sleep describes a private meeting with the founder of one of Nomad's investee companies who articulated the opposite orientation:

> "If you want to be successful, and we do, then you have to be willing to be misunderstood, and do things that do not seem sensible to most people… if you come into the office thinking how you are going to beat number one, two or three in the industry, then our firm is the wrong place for you. We start with the customer and work backwards." — Nomad investee founder (cited Letter 17, December 2009; attributed to Jeff Bezos in meeting context)

The phrase **"divine discontent"** captures the internal compass of such firms: a permanent dissatisfaction with the status quo that keeps the firm improving irrespective of what competitors do. This is the structural opposite of a locker room culture — it is outward-focused (toward the customer) rather than inward-focused (toward the league table).

### Equity Portfolios Constructed on the Same Principles
Sleep makes explicit that the locker room problem is not limited to the credit markets:

> "Such a ruse does not just exist in the credit markets: many equity portfolios are constructed using similar principles."

The "safe" diversified portfolio, sold on the basis that risk is reduced by owning 200 stocks, is the equity market equivalent of the mortgage securitisation trust. In both cases, complexity and the language of prudence are used to disguise the absence of genuine analysis.

### The Investment Implication
Locker room culture creates exploitable mispricings — but only for investors genuinely outside the bubble. Sleep's argument: "it helps to be outside the bubble to see the bubble." Nomad's patient, long-horizon structure places Sleep and Zakaria outside the incentive system that drives the locker room culture, which in turn allows them to own companies that exhibit the opposite — see [[Character_as_Moat]].

The alternative investment approach: "make a meaningful, long-term investment [which] breaks the in-out, get-rich-quick spell of the salesman (and, let's face it, the salesman in one's own head!)." — Letter 17, December 2009
