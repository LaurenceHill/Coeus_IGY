# Costco Wholesale

## Summary
Costco Wholesale is a US membership-based warehouse club retailer and [[Nomad_Investment_Partnership]]'s most thoroughly analysed holding. First discussed in Letter 3 (December 2002) at ~3.1% of the fund, it had grown to **~10% of fund assets by Letter 7 (December 2004)** — Sleep's largest single position and the best example he has found of the [[Scale_Economies_Shared]] investment model. Sleep describes it as "a cost disciplined, intellectually honest, high product integrity, perpetual motion machine trading at a discount to value" and names it an explicit [[Terminal_Portfolio]] candidate.

## Explanation

> **Coverage:** First detailed in Letter 3 (December 2002). Deep-dive analysis in Letter 7 (December 2004) — Sleep's longest treatment of any investment. Robustness Ratio framework applied in Letter 8 (June 2005). Updated through Letter 8.

### The Business Model
Costco operates a **membership warehouse club**:
- Members pay an annual fee (standard US&#36;45 at the time) for access to the stores.
- Costco marks up branded goods at exactly **14%** and private label at **15%** — no exceptions, every day.
- The membership fee covers essentially all profit; the retail operation covers costs.

This creates a powerful and honest consumer proposition: the fee buys access to guaranteed lowest prices. The customer knows the mark-up, the contract is transparent, and trust is built over time.

### The Sinegal Pricing Discipline
Jim Sinegal (Costco's founder and CEO) was legendary for refusing to break the pricing rule. Sleep recounts a story told to him by a company director: Costco bought 2 million designer jeans at an all-in cost of US&#36;22 per pair — US&#36;10 less than past pricing. A buyer suggested taking a higher gross margin since "no one would know." Sinegal refused:

> "If I let you do it this time, you will do it again." — Jim Sinegal

This is the living embodiment of [[Owner_Oriented_Management]] and the long-term thinking that characterises the best franchise businesses. Short-term margin expansion at the cost of customer trust would destroy the compounding model.

### Self-Reinforcing Loop
The EDLP (Every Day Low Pricing) model creates a virtuous cycle:
1. Very low prices attract and retain members.
2. Membership fee → essentially all profit.
3. Savings from scale and purchasing → passed back to customers as lower prices.
4. Lower prices → more members → more scale → more savings.

Sleep describes this as "retail's version of perpetual motion."

### The High-Low Contrast
Sleep contrasts Costco with typical retailers who use **high-low pricing** (promotional discounting). This creates consumer confusion: Is the tissue worth US&#36;5 or US&#36;4? The customer feels exploited at full price. High-low erodes trust; EDLP builds it.

### Growth Potential
At the time of purchase:
- 21 Costco stores in Washington State (2% of US population) → implied ~1,000 potential US locations (vs. 284 then).
- 200 potential UK stores (vs. 14 then).
- UK already had logistics/warehousing for 40 locations.
- At 10% growth per annum: 13 more years of runway.
- Comparable: Home Depot had 1,500 stores at the time.

### Valuation — Letter 3 (December 2002)
> ⚠️ **Snapshot — initial purchase.** Updated through Letter 8; see Letter 7 valuation section below.

| Metric | Value |
|---|---|
| Peak share price (2000) | US&#36;55 |
| Nomad purchase price (initial) | US&#36;30 |
| Sleep's intrinsic value estimate | US&#36;50+ |
| Reason for decline | Margin compression from new distribution centres (temporary) |

The margin compression was investment-related — capacity being built for future growth. Sleep viewed this as precisely the kind of near-term earnings noise that creates long-term opportunity. See [[Short_Termism]].

### Scale Economies Shared — Letter 7 (December 2004)
By Letter 7, Costco had grown to **~10% of fund assets** (average purchase price US&#36;39 — a blend of the original US&#36;30 and US&#36;40 paid when drawing down the subscription queue). Sleep uses Costco as the canonical demonstration of the [[Scale_Economies_Shared]] model.

> "In the office we have a white board on which we have listed the (very few) investment models that work and that we can understand. Costco is the best example we can find of one of them: scale efficiencies shared." — Letter 7, December 2004

The business economics at this date (revenues US&#36;47.2bn, year to August 2004):
- 12% markup on merchandising costs
- Operating costs (SG&A): 9.8% of sales
- Free cash flow as cash cow: ~US&#36;1.17bn p.a.
- Fixed assets: US&#36;9.6bn (depreciated US&#36;7.3bn) → **~12% FCF yield on fixed assets**
- Oldest stores earn ~16.5% on assets; youngest earn ~6% → ~50% rise in aggregate profits as store base matures

**Valuation range (Letter 7):**
| Growth assumption | Implied share price |
|---|---|
| 5–6% p.a. (modest) | ~US&#36;39 (average purchase price) |
| 7% p.a. | ~US&#36;43 |
| 10% p.a. (central case) | ~US&#36;62 |
| 15% p.a. | US&#36;85–90 |

Sleep considers 10% growth the central case: normalised growth of 13% before new store openings. If convergence to 10% discount takes five years, the shares need to rise at ~20% p.a. from US&#36;39. Sleep explicitly says the position at ~10% of fund is **too small per the Kelly Criterion** — "I may have already made my first mistake with Costco. From not buying enough." See [[Kelly_Criterion]].

Three market heuristics incorrectly applied to Costco (see [[Valuation_Heuristics]]):
1. "Low margins" (1.7% vs Wal-Mart 3.6%) — the margin *is* the point; deliberate under-earning extends franchise life
2. "Expensive at 24x earnings" — at Wal-Mart margins it would trade at 11x
3. "Cost problem" — rising costs are infrastructure investment for growth, not structural

### Robustness Ratio — Letter 8 (June 2005)
Sleep applies the [[Robustness_Ratio]] framework to Costco, finding a **~5:1 ratio** (customers and employees receive ~5× what shareholders retain per cardholder). The Wall Street view — that this is "retailing's equivalent of collectivism" — misses that this is precisely what makes the moat unassailable.

> ⚠️ **Snapshot — Letter 8 (June 2005).** Costco's share price had been essentially flat for six years at this point. Sleep's concern: management beginning to "gently tinker with the division of benefits in favour of shareholders" under Wall Street pressure. He argues the correct response is share buybacks, not margin expansion.

The competitive implication: matching Costco's prices and pay would cost Sam's Club ~US&#36;2.15bn annually — not insignificant even for Wal-Mart. "Game over."
