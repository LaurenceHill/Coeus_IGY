# Xerox Corporation

## Summary
Xerox is a US manufacturer of high-end printers and copiers discussed extensively in [[Nomad_Investment_Partnership]] Letter 3 (December 2002). It is a textbook [[Accounting_Manipulations|accounting manipulation]] case study and a deep value workout: market capitalisation had fallen to 7% of its peak due to an SEC investigation into lease accounting fraud, but free cash flow was entirely unaffected. Sleep purchased shares at US&#36;6 against an estimated intrinsic value of ~US&#36;14.

## Explanation

### Business Overview
Xerox had ~US&#36;17bn annual revenues and a ~70% share in high-end printers (US&#36;100,000+). It was a profitable but modest-growth business — ~5% revenue growth — which made its ambitious earnings-per-share targets of 15% per annum impossible to achieve organically.

### The Manipulation: SFAS 13 Lease Accounting
To hit Wall Street's growth expectations, management exploited **SFAS 13 (Statement of Financial Accounting Standards No. 13 — "Accounting for Leases")** to pull future revenues forward. Sleep explains the mechanism with unusual clarity — see [[Accounting_Manipulations]] for the full concept.

In short: Xerox bundled equipment, service, and financing into multi-year lease contracts. By inflating the "equipment value" portion of the bundle, the contract could be classified as a sale-type lease rather than an operating lease, allowing the full (inflated) equipment value to be recognised as revenue immediately rather than spread over the lease term.

> "A double whammy of inflated revenues and profits recognised early." — Letter 3, December 2002

Over five years, US&#36;6.4bn of revenues were incorrectly booked out of cumulative revenues approaching US&#36;90bn (~7%). The manipulation triggered an SEC investigation, auditor replacement, and near-closure of the commercial paper market to the firm.

### The Crucial Insight: Cash Flow Unchanged
Throughout the manipulation and the aftermath, **cash flow was completely unaffected**:
- Customers continued paying their monthly bills as contracted.
- The only thing changed was the accounting timing of revenue recognition.
- New auditors, ironically, found that the restated accounts actually *understated* profit going forward — because reversing the inflated lease classification now deferred revenues that legitimately belonged to the period.

> "Cash flow is unchanged" — Sleep's refrain throughout this analysis.

This is the core of Sleep's deep value methodology: reported earnings can be manipulated; cash flow is a much harder number to fake. See [[Margin_of_Safety]].

### Valuation
> ⚠️ **Snapshot — Letter 3 (December 2002).** Xerox not revisited in subsequent letters. Final state as of Letter 3.

| Metric | Value |
|---|---|
| Peak market cap (earlier years) | ~US&#36;70/share implied |
| Price at Nomad purchase | US&#36;6/share |
| Sleep's intrinsic value estimate | ~US&#36;14/share |
| Free cash flow | ~US&#36;1bn per annum after interest, tax, and maintenance capex |
| Implied FCF yield at US&#36;6 | ~22% |
| Net debt (adjusted) | ~US&#36;2bn (US&#36;17bn gross offset by US&#36;15bn receivables + cash) |
| R&D capitalised value estimate | ~US&#36;7bn (50% above market cap at purchase) |
| Avg. holding period of Xerox stock (market) | Under 4 months |

The low holding period is Sleep's evidence that almost no professional investor was thinking about multi-year business value. This is [[Short_Termism]] at work, and the source of the opportunity.

### Risk
Sleep identified one specific risk: dilutive share issuance. Bankers were urging management to issue equity. Sleep was actively lobbying management to resist:

> "The effect is that we are no longer confident of valuing Monsanto with any certainty and the prudent thing to do was to sell our shares." — this quote is from Monsanto; the Xerox risk was dilution specifically.

> "[…] something we are lobbying management to resist, hopefully more effectively than the bankers who are egging them on." — Letter 3, on share issuance at Xerox.

### Key Lessons
1. **Accounting noise ≠ economic reality.** Reported earnings manipulated; cash flow intact.
2. **Short-term investor focus creates deep value.** Under-4-month average holding period means few buyers are doing 5-year analysis.
3. **Debt that is matched by receivables is different from unmatched leverage.** Xerox's "US&#36;17bn debt" was almost entirely offset by trade receivables — the leases were effectively self-financing.
4. **R&D as an unrecognised asset.** Xerox spent ~US&#36;1bn/year on R&D regardless of the accounting chaos. Capitalised over a reasonable period, this alone was worth more than the market cap.
