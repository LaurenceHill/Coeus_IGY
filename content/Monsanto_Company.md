# Monsanto Company

## Summary
Monsanto is a US agricultural biotechnology company (genetically modified seeds and fertilisers) that was purchased and then sold by [[Nomad_Investment_Partnership]] at approximately break-even in 2002. It is cited as an **analytical error** — Sleep missed a contingent liability buried in the footnotes of SEC filings. The position is a case study in the importance of exhaustive footnote reading and the limits of valuation certainty when legal liabilities are unbounded.

## Explanation

### Why Nomad Bought It
Monsanto had strong business economics, good growth potential, capable management, and a low share price — Sleep's words: "right up Nomad's street." On all observable metrics it met Nomad's criteria.

### The Corporate History (The Complexity)
Monsanto's corporate structure had been repeatedly reorganised:
1. Original Monsanto included the seed business, a commodity chemical business (Solutia), and the G.D. Searle drug operations.
2. In 1997, **Solutia** was spun off to Monsanto shareholders.
3. In early 2000, **Pharmacia Corp** acquired the remaining Monsanto operations, then spun out the seed business to retain the Searle drug operations.

This created a chain of entities with interlocking liabilities.

### The Missed Liability: Solutia PCB Indemnification
Solutia owned a **PCB (polychlorinated biphenyl) plant in Alabama** that faced potential environmental clean-up costs and punitive damages. Solutia's own balance sheet was weak — heavy healthcare liabilities and debt. Any award exceeding Solutia's capacity would pass to the parent (Pharmacia). But Monsanto had **indemnified Pharmacia** for any environmental or punitive damages arising from Solutia's operations.

The crucial detail: this indemnification was **buried in the notes to Monsanto's accounts** in SEC filings. Sleep missed it on initial analysis and only discovered it on re-reading company filings.

> "It was only on reading recent company filings with the SEC that the indemnification buried in the notes to the accounts came to our attention." — Letter 2, June 2002

### Why This Forced a Sale
Once discovered, Sleep could not value Monsanto with confidence:
- The size of potential damages was unknown.
- Legal counsel could not determine where an award would rank in Solutia's creditor hierarchy if it went bankrupt.
- Therefore the contingent liability flowing back to Monsanto was unknowable — it could be zero or it could be existential.

> "Value for us, the Monsanto shareholder, may be decided by a judge in an Alabama court, and on that basis we are nervous." — Letter 2, June 2002

Sleep's response was disciplined: if you cannot value something with reasonable confidence, you cannot own it regardless of how good the business looks otherwise. He sold at approximately the purchase price. The shares subsequently fell to half the exit price — validating the decision.

### The Lesson: Read Every Footnote
This is the most direct illustration in the early letters of why Sleep describes the investment process as detective work — see [[Franchise_Businesses]]. The business quality was high, the valuation looked attractive, but the critical fact was in the footnotes, not in the headline financial statements.

It also illustrates the asymmetry of analytical errors: missing a positive factor costs you opportunity; missing a negative contingent liability can cost you the investment. The downside case demands more scrutiny than the upside.

### Contrast with Conseco
Both Conseco and Monsanto are acknowledged errors, but they are different in kind:
- **Conseco** was a bad outcome despite reasonable analysis — a business that failed.
- **Monsanto** was an analytical miss — good business, but a contingent liability Sleep initially overlooked. Sleep caught the error before it cost money.
