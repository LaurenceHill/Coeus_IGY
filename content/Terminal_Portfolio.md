# Terminal Portfolio ("Super High-Quality Thinkers")

## Summary
The Terminal Portfolio is [[Nick_Sleep]]'s concept of an ideal end-state for [[Nomad_Investment_Partnership]]: a concentrated group of "super high-quality thinkers" — businesses so intellectually honest, so disciplined in capital allocation, and with such durable competitive advantages, that shareholders could hold them indefinitely and trust their capital to be well-allocated without active trading. First articulated in Letter 6 (June 2004), it represents the direction Nomad is heading, not yet its current state. It is also the conceptual ancestor of the scale-economic-moat thinking that dominates the later letters.

## Explanation

### The Definition
At the time of Letter 6, Sleep kept an internal office list of **fifteen businesses** — the "super high-quality thinkers." Entry criteria were narrow:

> "Entry is reserved for the intellectually honest and economically rational, but that alone is not enough. There are many companies that do the right thing when their backs are against the wall, and this list excludes those temporarily attending church. The anointed few are there because they have chosen to **out-think their competition** and allocate capital over many years with discipline to reinforce their firm's competitive advantage." — Letter 6, June 2004

The key disqualifier is **performance under pressure only** — doing the right thing when forced to is not the same as choosing to do the right thing when the pressure is off. The list is for companies that *want* to do it right, not those that *have to*.

### Why "Terminal"
Sleep uses "terminal" in the sense of a final destination — if these businesses are found at the right price, the *terminal action* is to hold them forever. Shareholders could "abdicate their right to trade stock (allocate capital themselves) sure in the knowledge that their capital will be well allocated for years to come within the businesses."

This is a profound reframing of the investor's role: rather than continuously reallocating capital to the highest expected return, the goal is to find businesses that do the reallocation *for you*, better than you could do it externally.

### The Gap Between Current Portfolio and Terminal Portfolio
Sleep explicitly names this gap and explains why it exists:

> "The question is, why is this list not the same as the current Nomad portfolio?"

His answer: **price**. The terminal portfolio businesses are typically well-understood, well-owned, and priced at or above fair value. The current Nomad portfolio is built on the 50-cents-on-the-dollar discipline (see [[Margin_of_Safety]]) which often requires some element of distress, misunderstanding, or cyclical trough. Most "super high-quality thinkers" don't get cheap enough often enough. Sleep's response is to do the work *now* so he is ready when they do:

> "The trick is to do the work today, so that we are ready." — Letter 6, June 2004

Even Berkshire Hathaway — "with its stalwart long-term shareholder base" — was demonstrably half-priced in early 2000. Marathon bought shares. The opportunity exists; it just requires patience.

### Named Examples (Letter 6)

> ⚠️ **Snapshot — Letter 6 (June 2004).** Nomad's current overlap with the terminal portfolio list (e.g. Costco position size) reflects this date. All letters now processed. Terminal portfolio concept evolved but no updated constituent list was provided.

Sleep does not name all fifteen, but identifies:
- **[[Costco_Wholesale]]**: Already a meaningful Nomad holding (first entered at ~3.1% of fund in Letter 3; position grown by June 2004). Described as "one of the wonderful compounding machines" already represented in the portfolio. The EDLP model, membership economics, and management culture make it a canonical terminal portfolio candidate.
- **National Indemnity** (Berkshire Hathaway subsidiary): An insurance company with the discipline to *stop writing business* when pricing is poor — even if revenues decline by 80% for years. Sleep sees this as the purest form of capital discipline: refusing to grow when growth would destroy returns.

### "Want To" vs "Have To"
A second key distinction Sleep draws:

> "There are only two reasons companies behave well. Because they want to, and because they have to. Our preference is to invest in those that **want to**." — Letter 6, June 2004

Companies that behave well only under external pressure (activist shareholders, near-bankruptcy, regulatory scrutiny) are excluded. The terminal portfolio consists only of companies with an *intrinsic* orientation toward honest capital allocation.

### Relationship to "Permanent Holdings"
The Terminal Portfolio concept is Sleep's first explicit articulation of what later letters describe as "near-permanent holdings" — the endgame of Nomad's evolution from cigar-butt investing toward very long-duration, high-quality compounders. This transition is described in the 2021 preamble to the IGY Foundation edition of the letters.

### Capital Discipline vs. Growth
Letter 6 explicitly challenges the assumption that good capital allocation means growth:

> "Good capital allocation takes many forms and does not necessarily require a firm to grow. The Partnership's successful investment in [[Stagecoach_Group]] has been due to the firm's **shrink strategy**, not its growth." — Letter 6, June 2004

National Indemnity standing back from writing insurance for years — revenues declining 80% — is celebrated as a model of discipline. The pressure to grow (from within, from Wall Street, from short-term shareholders) is almost always present; the terminal portfolio businesses are those that can resist it.
