# Information as Food

## Summary
[[Nick_Sleep]]'s framework from Letter 22 (June 2012) for how to manage the information problem in investing. Drawing on JP Rangaswami's analogy between information and food, Charles Darwin's model of intensive thinking over data collection, and the "Expensive Tissue Hypothesis" from biological anthropology, Sleep argues that the highest-value information is that with the longest shelf life — and that the investor's task is to build a "data diet" centred on near-axiomatic structural truths rather than on the constant consumption of perishable market data.

## Explanation

### The Darwin / Attenborough Model
Letter 22 opens with David Attenborough being introduced on BBC Radio 4's Desert Island Discs as the person who "has seen more of the world than any person who has ever lived." Attenborough's modest response:

> "Perhaps the greatest naturalist that ever lived and had more effect on our thinking than anybody, Charles Darwin, only spent four years travelling and the rest of the time thinking." — David Attenborough (cited Letter 22, June 2012)

Sleep's inference: the human frontal cortex — charged with rational thought and information processing — can make more sense of the world, given enough time, than the senses themselves can collect. Darwin's four years of travel produced the insight; the subsequent decades of thinking produced *The Origin of Species*. The ratio of thinking to data collection was approximately **10:1 in favour of thinking**.

Applied to investing: in an information-soaked world, the temptation is to treat constant data collection as the job. Sleep takes the opposite view — de-emphasise data collection and think. "When we study truly great businesses, we find that very often it has been simple human attributes that have led to their success… and the reason you have these feelings, and the stimuli that produce them, have hardly changed in millennia."

### Rangaswami's Analogy
Journalist and technologist JP Rangaswami proposes treating information the same way one treats food. Sleep finds this "wonderfully provocative":

- **Hunter-gatherers vs. farmers**: in food, we choose to roam or put up fences (ownership); in information, is this not what patent and copyright law is about?
- **Distilled nutrition vs. smorgasbord**: a beef steak (a single conclusion) vs. raw ingredients (underlying data); the same choice exists when an analyst serves a recommendation vs. provides the underlying evidence.
- **Quality standards**: food has labelling; information on television does not — fact and fiction can be "deliberately fused" (docudramas, mockumentaries, The Da Vinci Code). One solution: label information for its **fact content** the way we label food for its **fat content**.
- **Sell-by date**: next quarter's earnings are worthless after next quarter. Information decays.
- **Over-consumption**: Clay Shirky — "there is no such animal as information overload, only filter failure." Rangaswami's extension: "what would happen if an individual had thirty-one days nonstop Fox News?" Data diets and information exercise prevent the build-up of "toxins and disease."

### The Expensive Tissue Hypothesis
Sleep cites biological anthropologists Leslie Aiello and Peter Wheeler's "Expensive Tissue Hypothesis": for a given body size, a primate's metabolic rate is relatively static. What varies is the balance of tissues. The most metabolically expensive tissues — the nervous system (including the brain) and the digestive system — trade off against each other. In humans: large brains, small stomachs. In pigs: the opposite. A larger brain requires a smaller gut — and the larger brain provides the thinking power to organise the world so as not to require constant grazing.

Sleep's application: the brain that thinks deeply about a few things is more valuable than the brain that grazes constantly on data. The fund manager who reads 200 annual reports per year and thinks shallowly about each is the pig. The fund manager who reads 10 and thinks for months about each is the human.

### The Shelf-Life Hierarchy
The practical output of the framework is a **hierarchy of information by shelf life**:

| Information type | Shelf life | Weight in Nomad's analysis |
|---|---|---|
| Near-axiomatic structural truths (SES model, customer relationships) | Decades | Highest |
| Business culture and character | Years | High |
| Competitive position, market share dynamics | Quarters to years | Medium |
| Quarterly earnings, macro events, political news | Days to weeks | Lowest / near zero |

> "The information that Zak and I weigh most heavily in thinking about a firm is that which has the longest shelf life, with the highest weighting going to information that is almost axiomatic: it is, in our opinion, the most valuable information." — Letter 22, June 2012

The near-axiomatic information Sleep has in mind is the scale-economics-shared model: the insight that if you lower costs and give the savings to customers, the customer reciprocates and the moat deepens. This insight was as true in Henry Ford's era as it is today — it has not decayed.

### The Ford 1908–1924 Chart
Letter 22 reproduces a 1927 Franklin Institute report recommending Henry Ford for its Elliott Cresson Medal, showing two lines:
- **Line A**: Ford production volumes, rising from 0 to just over 2 million cars per annum (1908–1924)
- **Line B**: Cost to the consumer of the Model T Ford, falling from US&#36;950 to ~US&#36;300

This is the [[Scale_Economies_Shared]] model in its earliest documented form — a century before Amazon. Sleep's observation: "The business model that built the Ford empire a hundred years ago is the same that built Sam Walton's (Wal-Mart) in the 1970s, Herb Kelleher's (Southwest Airlines) in the 1990s or Jeff Bezos's (Amazon.com) today."

The longevity of the model is itself axiomatic: as Bezos put it, "I can't imagine that in ten years from now customers are going to say: I really love Amazon, but I wish their prices were a little higher." The customer preference for low prices is not a trend — it is a permanent feature of human nature. Information with that shelf life is the most valuable of all.

### The Inversion: Simplicity Under Complexity
The framework inverts the industry's approach to research. The conventional view: more data = better decisions. Sleep's view: more thinking about fewer, longer-lived data points = better decisions.

This connects directly to [[Inactivity_as_Strategy]]: the fund manager who is inactive in trading can be intensely active in thinking. The UK Top Gun sitting on his hands is not passive — he is directing cognitive resources away from action and toward understanding.

It also connects to [[Valuation_Heuristics]]: the investor who anchors on next quarter's earnings is consuming information with a very short shelf life. The investor who focuses on the structural relationship between a business and its customers is consuming information with a very long shelf life. Both call themselves researchers. The results diverge over time.
