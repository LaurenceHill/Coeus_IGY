# Per-Share Discipline (Share Count Stability)

## Summary
Per-share discipline refers to management's commitment to maintaining a stable share count — neither diluting existing owners through equity issuance nor distorting the per-share value record through excessive buybacks or option grants. [[Nick_Sleep]] uses stability of share count over time as a screen for **corporate character**: companies that resisted the temptations of the bubble era reveal something deep about their values and incentive structures.

## Explanation

### The Screen
In early 2003, Sleep commissioned a **company database search** — described as the first such search in his 12-year career — with a single criterion:

> *Companies with no increase or decrease in shares outstanding in the last ten years.*

The intent: find companies that had resisted every major fashion of the prior decade — share repurchases, stock option grants, equity issuance for acquisitions, and bubble-era equity fundraising. The result would be companies whose management had simply run the business, compounding value from a static ownership base.

The result in the US (market cap > US&#36;50m): **seven companies total**, of which Sleep already knew two — Fastenal and Wesco Financial. The extraordinary scarcity of this quality is itself a signal of how rare genuine per-share discipline is.

### Why Share Dilution Matters
Sleep argues that the long-term rate of US share issuance exceeds **3% per annum** — comparable to trend GDP growth but far exceeding population growth. Since something is usually "bought" with new shares (management options, acquisitions, equity raises), the debate often focuses on whether the thing purchased justified the dilution. Sleep's concern goes deeper:

- **Duration mismatch**: Incentive compensation (paying a manager with a one-year employment contract) is a transitory outcome. Newly issued shares may remain outstanding forever. Using permanent capital to buy transitory outcomes is irrational.
- **Short-term investors don't penalise it**: The dripping tap of share issuance is rarely turned off because short-term equity investors care little about dilution's long-term compounding effects. See [[Short_Termism]].
- **It reveals incentive misalignment**: Heavy share issuance often reflects management rewarding themselves at shareholders' expense. See [[Owner_Oriented_Management]].

### The Examples Found
**Fastenal** and **Wesco Financial** — both already known to Sleep before the search — were on the list. Both are businesses with unusual management cultures focused on compounding value per share.

**Erie Indemnity / Erie Family Life**: Sleep contacted this company (founded by H.O. Hirt in the 1920s) after it appeared on the screen. Management's response to being asked about share issuance:

> *"Hell, we have not issued a share since 1925!!!"*

H.O. Hirt's famous internal notice posted to staff:
> *"RULES / Are for INFANTS, INCOMPETENTS, / INCARCERATED CRIMINALS and IMBECILES / — NONE of WHOM should have any place in the ERIE FAMILY."*

Sleep uses this as the spirit of the per-share discipline ethic: run the business, don't follow fashions.

**Hershey Creamery**: Another stable-share-count business stumbled upon by the screen. So stubbornly private in culture that Sleep could not even obtain annual reports or proxy statements through normal channels — only by calling the CFO, who told him they were "mailed to shareholders." The analytical catch-22 (can't evaluate without the report; can't get the report unless already a shareholder) was treated by Sleep as itself revealing — a company resolutely independent of mind despite twenty years of bull market pressure around it.

### Link to [[Weetabix_Limited]]
Weetabix's share count has been essentially **unchanged since the 1930s**. Sleep explicitly connects this to per-share discipline: the firm "had resisted the fashions for share repurchase, stock options, and share issuance to fund growth" and thereby demonstrated the kind of corporate character that allows intrinsic value to compound undisturbed.

### The Two Strategies for Investors
Given that dilution is endemic, Sleep identifies two viable responses:
1. **Be a beneficiary of dilution** — invest in the more senior securities (bonds, preferred stock) that dilution serves, e.g. [[Lucent_Technologies_Preferred]].
2. **Own a business with a stable share base** capable of compounding value without diluting owners — e.g. [[Weetabix_Limited]].

### Link to Investment Mandate Breadth
Sleep's ability to pursue strategy (1) — buying preferred stock — requires a mandate that permits investment across the capital structure. Most fund managers are restricted to equities. This is Sleep's argument for Nomad's deliberately broad mandate.
