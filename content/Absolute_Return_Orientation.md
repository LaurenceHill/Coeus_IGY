# Absolute Return Orientation

## Summary
Absolute return orientation is a foundational principle of the [[Nomad_Investment_Partnership]]: the goal is to make real money in absolute terms, not merely to outperform an index. Beating an index is welcomed as a by-product, but it is not the primary objective. This framing has profound downstream consequences for portfolio construction, patience, and the type of investor the fund can accept.

## Explanation

### The Core Distinction
Most professional fund managers are measured against a benchmark. This creates a powerful structural bias: underperforming the index (even with positive absolute returns) is career-threatening, which causes managers to hug the index, act short-term, and avoid unfashionable positions. [[Nick_Sleep]] and [[Qais_Zakaria]] explicitly rejected this framing from the outset.

> "Nomad is an absolute return fund and reference here to an index is simply to place our performance, short term as it is, in context." — Letter 1, December 2001

> "We expect to beat the index handsomely over time, but only as a byproduct of our absolute return orientation." — Letter 1, December 2001

### The Buffett Asymmetry Insight
In Letter 2 (June 2002), Sleep quotes directly from the **1960 Buffett Partnership letter** to articulate how absolute-return investors should expect their relative performance to behave:

> "I have pointed out that any superior record which we might accomplish should not be expected to be evidenced by a relatively constant advantage in performance compared to the Average. Rather it is likely that if such an advantage is achieved, it will be through better-than-average performance in stable or declining markets and average, or perhaps even poorer-than-average performance in rising markets." — Warren Buffett, 1960

Sleep notes this is "axiomatic" for value investors: during booms, absolute-return value investors will look poor relative to the index; after booms they will look good. The investor must understand and accept this pattern.

### Why Indices Are a Poor Hurdle
Sleep also observes that in the early 2000s, the absolute return of major indices was expected to be modest over the coming decade — making the index a low bar. This view was vindicated: the MSCI World fell **-16.3%** from Nomad's inception (September 2001) through December 2002, while Nomad was up +11.6% over the same period (per the performance table in [[Nomad_Investment_Partnership]]).

### Link to Patience
Absolute return orientation is inseparable from [[Patience_as_Competitive_Advantage]]. If investors demand short-term relative performance, the manager cannot adopt an absolute return posture. The partnership structure of Nomad was specifically designed to attract investors with long time horizons who accepted this framing.

### Three Mistakes of Index-Relative Thinking — Letter 11
By Letter 11 (December 2006), Sleep made the rejection of index-relative thinking fully explicit. Nomad underperformed the MSCI World in 2006 (+13.6% vs +20.1%) and Sleep used this as an opportunity to articulate why this should not matter:

> "So, there is no confusion: the index is not our benchmark. Zak and I spend no time at all thinking about the index (indeed we were unaware of the index return until I set about writing this letter)." — Letter 11, December 2006

He identifies three downstream mistakes that follow from viewing the index as a "risk-free home":

1. **Circle of competence violations** — the requirement to have an opinion on everything inside the index, regardless of what you actually understand
2. **Opportunity cost blindness** — unwillingness to invest in better opportunities outside the index
3. **Over-diversification** — the mechanical replication of index holdings, destroying genuine conviction

Sleep's reframing: "The index is one of many opportunity sets." An index fund is not risk-free; it is a particular bet on a particular set of securities. This year that opportunity set beat Nomad. It may do so again. What matters is the destination, not the annual fluctuation.

> "A rational mind understands that it is the destination that is important, and if we have some skill in picking stocks... our destination will be some way ahead of the index." — Letter 11, December 2006

### Why Index Funds Sell
Sleep is clear-eyed about the commercial reality: index-relative funds are the industry norm "because they sell." The client does not trust the manager with the keys to the Ferrari — so they settle for a vehicle that moves with the herd. Sleep describes this as "a ghastly Faustian pact." The unconstrained mandate (see [[Principal_Agent_Conflict]] and [[Sleep_Zakaria_and_Company]]) is the alternative: a smaller, more confident, less commercially acceptable approach.

### Benchmarks Named for Aspiration
Instead of an index, Sleep names the investors whose multi-decade records he aspires to approach: Ruane, Tweedy, Klarman, Whitman, Hawkins, Miller, Schloss, Berkowitz. These are absolute-return value investors, not index-trackers.

## Potential Contradictions / Tensions
- Sleep does include index comparisons in every letter, despite downplaying their significance. This is acknowledged: the comparisons are "simply to place performance in context." No contradiction with the absolute return philosophy — just transparency.
