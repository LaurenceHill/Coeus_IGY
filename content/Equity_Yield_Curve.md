# Equity Yield Curve

## Summary
The equity yield curve is [[Nick_Sleep]]'s concept that patience has measurable value in equity investing: just as bond yields are higher for longer maturities (compensating for duration risk), equity returns increase with holding period — but for a fundamentally different reason. Introduced formally in Letter 11 (December 2006), and previewed in an interview for the *Outstanding Investor Digest*, it is one of the most important structural arguments for [[Patience_as_Competitive_Advantage]] in the Nomad letters.

## Explanation

### The Core Argument
In the bond market, longer maturities carry higher yields to compensate investors for two risks: the risk that principal will not be repaid, and the risk of inflation eroding its value. In equities, Sleep argues, the mechanism works differently:

> "In our opinion business outcomes can be more predictable several years out than they are in the near term." — Letter 11, December 2006

The near term in equities is dominated by noise — sentiment shifts, macro factors, short-term earnings misses — which have little bearing on long-run business value. But over a multi-year holding period, the underlying business reality (revenue growth, capital allocation, competitive moat widening) tends to reassert itself against near-term noise. Therefore:

> "The return from investing in shares can be both increased **and** de-risked by time." — Letter 11, December 2006

This is a counterintuitive inversion of conventional finance theory (which treats longer duration as higher risk). Sleep argues that for businesses with durable economics, extended holding periods *reduce* uncertainty while improving returns — the opposite of what bond math implies.

### Blind Spot in Academia
Sleep suggests conventional economics has a structural bias against this insight: the dominant methodology is to take observations over short time periods, treating cause and effect as if they sit on top of each other. Habits form over years. Coca-Cola's brand value is the product of a century of advertising spend — a relationship that no short-horizon study can capture.

### Portfolio Evidence — Nomad's Own Yield Curve
Sleep provides direct evidence from Nomad's portfolio in Letter 11:

| Holding cohort | Annualized return pattern |
|---|---|
| Purchased last year | "Mixed affair — several losses" |
| 1–2 years | Lower |
| 2–3 years | Higher |
| 3–4 years | Higher again |
| 4+ years | Superior annualized returns |

The curve is steep: **over 1,000 basis points per annum** of incremental return between adjacent cohorts.

Sleep acknowledges two biases in this data:
1. **Survivorship bias** — Conseco is excluded; the losses are gone.
2. **Opportunity set bias** — 2002 was a better vintage than 2006; early buys had wider margins of safety.

But even allowing for these, the steepness of the curve is real enough to be informative.

### Implication: Nomad is a Young Portfolio
At the time of Letter 11, the weighted average holding period across the portfolio was just over **one year**. Sleep's interpretation:

> "Nomad is a young portfolio, perhaps one fifth of the way through its normal life expectancy." — Letter 11, December 2006

This means the portfolio is currently at the short, competitive end of its own yield curve — where other investors (average mutual fund holding period < 1 year) are most active and competition is greatest. If the yield curve holds, the best years lie ahead.

### The Holding Period Gap
A concrete measure of Nomad's differentiation from its peers:

> Excluding Berkshire Hathaway (illiquid), Jardine Matheson, and Next Media (both investor-educated), other investors hold stocks in Nomad's portfolio for on average **twenty weeks**. Nomad expects to own shares for around **two hundred and sixty weeks** — thirteen times longer.

This gap is not random. Most investors use accounting outputs as a guide to near-term price movements. Sleep and Zakaria focus on "inputs to future value moves" — the underlying business realities that will determine outcomes years out. These are completely different stimuli, and the holding period difference reflects it.

### Connection to Equity Yield Curve and Competition
The short end of the equity yield curve is crowded: all mutual fund managers chasing the same quarterly earnings data. The long end — multi-year business realities — is nearly empty. Nomad's competitive advantage is specifically located where competition is least.
