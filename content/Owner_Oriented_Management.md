# Owner-Oriented Management

## Summary
Owner-oriented management is one of the three core investment criteria of the [[Nomad_Investment_Partnership]]. It refers to management teams who think and act like long-term business owners — allocating capital for durable value creation, aligning their personal financial interests with shareholders, and resisting the temptation to grow for growth's sake, manage for short-term earnings, or enrich themselves at the expense of owners.

## Explanation

### Why It Matters
[[Nick_Sleep]] and [[Qais_Zakaria]] understood that finding a cheap business is insufficient if management will destroy the value discount before the market can close it. Owner-oriented management is the mechanism by which intrinsic value is protected and extended. It also reduces agency risk — the risk that insiders and managers pursue their own interests at shareholders' expense.

### Indicators Sleep Watches For
Sleep identifies owner-orientation through several observable behaviours:

**Positive signals:**
- **Insider buying** — directors purchasing shares with their own money. Letter 3 notes that "nearly half of our companies (by number, more by value) are buying back debt or repurchasing shares and at two thirds of our firms there has been notable insider buying."
- **Share buybacks and debt repurchase** — returning capital when the shares are cheap rather than pursuing dilutive acquisitions.
- **Founder involvement** — families or founders who retain large stakes (e.g., the France family at Nascar owning Nascar's sanctioning body; Matichon's family with 25% equity; Brian Souter at [[Stagecoach_Group]] with ~25% family stake).
- **Disciplined pricing** — e.g., Jim Sinegal at [[Costco_Wholesale]] insisting on the standard 14% mark-up even when buyers pushed for higher margins.
- **Cool heads under pressure** — management acting counter-cyclically (buying back bonds at a discount during credit market stress, resuming buybacks after indiscriminate selling).

**Negative signals (the opposite):**
- Earnings per share management at the expense of economic reality (Xerox, Letter 3).
- Debt-funded acquisitions at cycle peaks driven by hubris (Stagecoach / Coach USA).
- Growth mandates set to satisfy Wall Street rather than business economics.

### The Corporate Character Screen (Letter 4)
In Letter 4, Sleep formalises the search for management character through a direct database screen — see [[Per_Share_Discipline]]. The single criterion: companies with **no change in shares outstanding over the last ten years**. This filters for management who resisted every temptation of the bubble era — options, acquisitions, equity raises. Only seven US companies above US&#36;50m market cap qualified.

The sub-theme from that search is that corporate character often reveals itself through **small, quotidian decisions** rather than dramatic moments: Erie Indemnity hadn't issued a share since 1925; Hershey Creamery was so indifferent to outside capital that they only mailed annual reports to existing shareholders. These are the businesses Sleep is most excited by. The logic: management who have maintained discipline through decades of external pressure are far more likely to continue doing so than management who simply haven't yet been tested.

### The Alignment Structure at Nomad
Sleep himself operates by the same principle: Nomad's primary fee is a performance fee, with management fees kept minimal (10 bps). He notes that [[Marathon_Asset_Management]] insiders are the largest single group of investors in the fund — putting their own money where their analysis is.

### Quotes
> "…companies run by owner-oriented management and employing capital allocation strategies consistent with long term shareholder wealth creation. Finding all three is rare." — Letter 1, December 2001

> "A cool head under pressure is what is required." — Letter 3, December 2002 (on Georgica's management resuming buybacks after market overreaction)

### The Founder's Portfolio — Letter 12 (June 2007)
By June 2007, Sleep observes an emergent property of Nomad's portfolio that was not planned:

> "The fund is overwhelmingly (over eighty-five percent) invested in firms run by their founders or first-generation management." — Letter 12, June 2007

Crucially, this was not a pre-stated criterion. Sleep and Zakaria did not set out to build a "founder portfolio." They arrived at one as a **by-product of assessing management quality**:

> "Zak and I did not plan for this! We have ended up with a portfolio of owner-managed businesses as a by-product of our assessment of the quality of the people involved. In other words, these managers earned their way into the portfolio." — Letter 12, June 2007

The logic is structural. Founder-managed businesses naturally embody owner-orientation because there is no separation between ownership and management: the person making capital allocation decisions also bears the full economic consequence of those decisions. The [[Principal_Agent_Conflict]] is minimised at source. Sleep describes this as "the principal (founder) agent (trading oriented fund manager) conflict... the deep reality of the markets and probably the dominant characteristic of our careers."

This explains the long-term performance pattern: founders plan for five-year outcomes; the trading-oriented fund managers who set market prices focus on the next quarter's data. The mismatch between these time horizons is where Nomad's returns come from.

### The "Divine Discontent" Formula — Letter 17 (December 2009)
Letter 17 adds the strongest positive articulation of what owner-oriented management looks like from the inside. In a private meeting with a Nomad investee founder, Sleep records a statement that encapsulates the orientation:

> "If you want to be successful, and we do, then you have to be willing to be misunderstood, and do things that do not seem sensible to most people… if you come into the office thinking how you are going to beat number one, two or three in the industry, then our firm is the wrong place for you. We start with the customer and work backwards." — Nomad investee founder (cited Letter 17, December 2009)

The founder described his firm as benefiting from a **"divine discontent"** — a permanent dissatisfaction with the status quo that keeps the organisation improving irrespective of what the competition does. The internal compass points toward the customer, not the competitor. This is the structural opposite of the [[Locker_Room_Culture]].

Sleep's observation: fewer than **5% of listed firms** exhibit this orientation. Yet the vast majority of Nomad's portfolio does — not because Sleep set out to find it, but because it is a prerequisite for the kind of long-run structural advantage that makes a business cheap relative to its destination. See [[Character_as_Moat]].

### Two Modes of Investing — Letter 17
Sleep crystallises the distinction between two fundamental investment approaches:

1. **Buy cheap, sell at profit, repeat** — many decisions per year; returns from market mispricing; prone to reinvestment risk at every step.
2. **Buy great business at reasonable price, hold indefinitely** — one buy decision plus daily decisions *not* to sell; returns from the business's compounding; reinvestment risk effectively eliminated.

Sleep writes: "Almost no one does [the second] in part because it requires patience — and the locker room set does not do patience — but also because inactivity is the enemy of high fees."

By 2009, Sleep and Zakaria are explicitly "drifting toward inactivity" as judged by the industry — because the decision not to trade is itself an active decision, made daily.
