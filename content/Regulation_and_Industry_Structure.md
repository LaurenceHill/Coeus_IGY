# Regulation and Industry Structure

## Summary
[[Nick_Sleep]]'s critique, from Letter 25 (December 2013), of the direction of financial regulation and its structural effect on the investment industry. Sleep's argument is threefold: (1) regulation has grown in scope and number without improving its targeting — blanket rules penalise simple, honest operations while sophisticated bad actors adapt; (2) the resulting compliance burden will drive industry consolidation, which will paradoxically increase systemic fragility; (3) if genuine behavioural reform is the goal, the most effective lever is one regulators have not used: taxing short-term investing.

## Explanation

### The Regulatory Proliferation
Since Nomad's launch in 2001, the number of regulators claiming oversight of the Nomad ecosystem doubled to **four**, and the scope of regulation increased by approximately **one order of magnitude**. The succession of UK regulators alone: IMRO → SFA → FSA → FCA (Financial Conduct Authority). Sleep notes that the FCA may require parts of the Nomad ecosystem to re-register for authorisation to function as investment service providers.

The new regulatory character is qualitatively different from its predecessors: "Gone is the light touch of self-regulation and instead the mechanism, and perhaps the political will, exists to be far more controlling." Examples of new mandates: separation of risk management from portfolio management (undefined in the regulation); remuneration policies (but oddly silent on dividend payments). A contradictory treatment: the London investment advisor is treated as an offshore partnership's general partner for regulatory purposes but as a subcontracted adviser for taxation — "a contradiction that may not be long lived."

### The Targeting Problem
Sleep's central critique: the new rules arise from the behaviour of liars and thieves (he names Bernard Madoff explicitly), yet apply uniformly to everyone regardless of behaviour or complexity:

> "In essence we are required to now prove that we are not Bernard Madoff but, unlike Mr. Madoff, there is no potential for us to earn our way to a lighter regime through good behaviour. Nor is any allowance made for the fact that we invest for the very long term or that we are a simple fund. Indeed, Nomad is treated the same as a leveraged, long/short hedge fund complex that deals in exotica and trades constantly." — Letter 25, December 2013

This is analogous to traffic speed limits: they moderate all behaviour for the general good, regardless of the driver's actual risk profile. Sleep accepts this logic in principle ("we have lived with those restrictions quite happily — well, almost") but argues that blanket rules are a blunt instrument:

> "What is really required in order to bear down on the liars and cheats are empowered, detectives-cum-regulators asking questions of those whose practices don't sniff right." — Letter 25, December 2013

### The Consolidation Risk
The compliance burden falls disproportionately on small, simple investment boutiques — precisely the operations that, in Sleep's view, are not the source of any systemic problem. Over time, this barrier to entry will drive **industry consolidation**: expensive regulation is best absorbed at scale. Sleep flags this as a desired outcome from a regulatory standpoint ("regulation is expensive to do") but a dangerous one structurally, citing Nassim Taleb:

> "Consolidation will, in the end, bring with it an unintended, concomitant increase in market fragility, to paraphrase Nassim Taleb." — Letter 25, December 2013

The concern: if the proprietor has "abdicated responsibility to the point of not knowing what is going on, that is not healthy either." The Cambrian explosion of intermediary service providers — each adding a layer between capital and its ultimate deployment — is symptomatic of this abdication. See [[Principal_Agent_Conflict]].

### The Actual Solution: Tax Short-Term Investing
Sleep offers his preferred alternative to blanket regulation in a deliberate whisper:

> "And if you really want to change industry behaviour, I'll whisper this, **tax short term investing**." — Letter 25, December 2013

This is the most pointed policy prescription in the letters. The logic is direct: short-termism is the root cause of most financial industry dysfunction (see [[Short_Termism]]). It creates misaligned incentives, momentum trading, quarterly earnings management, and the exploitation of principal-agent conflicts. A tax on short-term turnover would directly target the behaviour rather than the actors, and would automatically exempt long-term investors — the opposite of what blanket regulation achieves.

### Regulation as a Factor in Nomad's Closure
In the Spring 2021 Postamble to the published collection, Sleep explicitly lists regulation as one reason for closing Nomad in early 2014:

> "The direction of regulation was certainly irksome and the tools of regulation unnecessarily blunt but, also, we wanted to feel that we did not have to justify actions, and inactions, on an ongoing basis to a revolving door of interested parties." — Postamble, Spring 2021

Regulation was not the only reason (see [[Nomad_Investment_Partnership]]), but it was a push factor: the cost and friction of complying with rules designed for a different kind of firm made the marginal value of continuing to operate the fund lower.

### The Halcyon Days
Sleep's epitaph for the era of simple investing: "The halcyon days of the budding stock picker running his friends and family partnership from the sunroom at home at 5505, Farnam Street, Omaha (an image so important to Zak and me) are long gone." 5505 Farnam Street is Warren Buffett's home address in Omaha, Nebraska — where Buffett ran his early partnerships before Berkshire Hathaway. Sleep uses it as a symbol of the simple, principal-aligned, low-overhead operation that regulation has made structurally difficult to sustain.
