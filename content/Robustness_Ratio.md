# Robustness Ratio

## Summary
The Robustness Ratio is [[Nick_Sleep]]'s framework for empirically measuring the strength and width of a business moat, introduced in the appendix to Letter 8 (June 2005) via the Global Investment Review (May 2005). It is defined as the combined financial benefit delivered to **customers and employees** divided by the benefit retained by **shareholders**. The ratio is most applicable to businesses whose customer proposition is price-based, as in [[Scale_Economies_Shared]] models like [[Costco_Wholesale]] and GEICO.

## Explanation

### The Concept
Sleep was struck by a passage in the 2005 Berkshire Hathaway Annual Report in which [[Warren_Buffett]] described how GEICO's scale economies were divided among three constituencies — customers, employees, and shareholders — with quantified amounts for each. This prompted Sleep to formalise what he had been observing informally at Costco.

The Robustness Ratio emerges from the insight that **moat strength is not measured by margin, but by how much value the business withholds from competition by giving it to others**. A company that retains all scale benefits as profit is theoretically easy to compete against — a rival need only offer the customer a marginal discount. A company that has already passed most of the benefits to the customer has set an extraordinarily high bar for competition.

### The Formula
> Robustness Ratio = (Value delivered to customers + Value delivered to employees) ÷ Value retained by shareholders

### Case Study: GEICO (1:1)
From the 2005 Berkshire Hathaway Annual Report:
- Customers saved ~US&#36;1 billion on premiums vs. next-cheapest carrier
- Employees received ~US&#36;191m profit-sharing bonus (24.3% of salary)
- Shareholders earned ~US&#36;1 billion underwriting profit (plus returns on US&#36;5–6bn float)

GEICO's robustness ratio is approximately **1:1** — for every dollar shareholders retain, about one dollar goes to customers and employees combined.

### Case Study: Costco (~5:1)
Per cardholder (cardholder = individual, ~2 per household):
- Average Costco spend: US&#36;1,100/year
- Cost to supply at Costco prices: US&#36;980
- Comparable cost at Kroger: ~US&#36;1,300; at Wal-Mart: ~US&#36;1,250
- **Net customer saving: ~US&#36;175/cardholder** (after deducting the ~US&#36;23 average fee cost)
- Employee "over-payment" vs. competitors: ~US&#36;26/cardholder (Costco wages/benefits ~55% above competition)
- Shareholder earnings: ~US&#36;32/cardholder pre-tax

> ⚠️ **Snapshot — Letter 8 (June 2005).** These figures represent Costco's economics at this date. Robustness Ratio not revisited with new figures in subsequent letters. Final state as of Letter 8.

Costco's robustness ratio is approximately **5:1** — for every dollar shareholders retain, roughly five dollars go to customers and employees.

### Implications of a High Ratio
1. **Moat width**: A rival wishing to match Costco would need to absorb ~US&#36;1.4bn in price cuts plus ~US&#36;750m in wage increases annually — a formidable barrier even for Wal-Mart.
2. **Under-earning**: High robustness ratios often signal that the company is deliberately under-earning relative to its potential. This creates long-duration investment opportunities for investors willing to look beyond reported earnings. Costco's share price had been essentially flat for six years when Sleep wrote this analysis.
3. **Vulnerability to culture drift**: A high ratio also signals that the business depends on management *choosing* to maintain the division. Any drift toward shareholder extraction — however slight — erodes the moat and, paradoxically, creates risk for the shareholders who appear to benefit.

### Warning: Culture Drift
Sleep explicitly warns that even a culture built over forty years is not immune to pressure from Wall Street's fixation on short-term EPS. At the time of Letter 8, Costco was already "gently tinkering with the division of benefits in favour of shareholders." Sleep's view: this is worrying and unnecessary. The correct response to a share price that has flatlined for six years is to **buy back shares** (growing free cash flow per share) rather than raise prices or cut wages.

### Ratio as Dynamic, Not Static
Sleep stresses the ratio is not a one-time measurement but must be tracked over time. Key dynamics:
- **Early in franchise development**: Disproportionate giving to customers is correct — referrals and repeat business are essential.
- **At maturity**: The bias can shift somewhat toward shareholders — but modestly.
- **Harvesting**: Companies that "harvest" excessively (Coca-Cola leaning on bottlers, Gillette cutting advertising) often enter "growth purgatory" — growth slows just as shareholders realise their mistake in valuing reported profitability over franchise health.

### Limitations
- The ratio is most meaningful for businesses built on a **price-based customer proposition** (Costco, GEICO). It is less applicable to advertising-driven brands like Nike where the mechanism of value delivery is different.
- A lower ratio (e.g., 1:1 at GEICO) is not inherently weaker than a higher one — the right ratio depends on the competitive dynamics of the industry.
- Falling ratios do not necessarily indicate a shrinking moat; they may simply mean the moat is not being made as wide as it could be.
