# Scaling Laws

## Summary
Scaling laws — the study of how biological and physical systems change as they grow — are introduced by [[Nick_Sleep]] in Letter 12 (June 2007) as a framework for evaluating whether a business has the "skeletal structure" to grow from a small firm to a dominant one without collapse. The concept comes from Geoff West's work at the [[Santa_Fe_Institute]]. Applied to investing, it asks: does this company's fundamental model become *more* efficient as it scales, or does growing complexity eventually cause it to buckle under its own weight?

## Explanation

### Biological Scaling Laws (Geoff West)
Geoff West at the Santa Fe Institute set out to answer two questions: why do smaller animals live shorter lives than larger ones, and why do humans live approximately one hundred years?

**The skeletal constraint:** As an organism increases in size, body-mass grows with volume (cubed), while skeletal shear strength only increases with bone width (squared — a power law of 3/2). Without a proportionately larger skeleton, mass soon overwhelms strength, and the organism collapses. Several overleveraged companies have followed a similar trajectory.

**Metabolic efficiency (Kleiber's Law):** Metabolic rates rise with body mass, but at a declining rate (a power law of 3/4). This means a larger animal is proportionately *more* metabolically efficient per unit of mass — there is an economy of scale. Heart rate also declines with mass.

**The Billion Heartbeats Insight:** Combining these two power laws: longevity increases with mass, and heart rate decreases with mass, so all mammals share approximately one billion heartbeats. A mouse uses its billion heartbeats in about four years (500 beats/min). An elephant uses its billion in seventy years (25 beats/min). Evolution has not changed this basic constraint.

**Why does the mouse heart beat so fast?** Because smaller animals have less body space to widen cross-sectional blood vessel area (which reduces viscous drag on blood flow), so the heart must work harder to maintain circulation. Larger animals benefit from greater circulatory efficiency.

### Applied to Companies
Sleep translates this biological framework into a question for investors: **does the company's basic building block scale efficiently, or does growing complexity overwhelm it?**

A business that wants to grow "from a mouse to an elephant" needs:
1. **Self-funding growth** — sufficient return on capital to reinvest without constant external financing
2. **Increasing barriers to entry with size** — the moat widens as the firm grows, not narrows
3. **A simple skeletal structure** — one that does not require re-engineering as scale increases

> "We want a skeletal structure that can support growth from mouse to elephant without too much skeletal re-engineering." — Letter 12, June 2007

### High Street Retail vs. Internet Retail
Sleep contrasts two skeletal structures directly:

**High street retail:**
- Goods: supplier → central warehouse → high street stores (expensive real estate, high operating costs)
- Service quality: a function of staff levels, helpfulness, product range, shop furnishings — all constantly variable, difficult to control, expensive to maintain
- At the most expensive end of the distribution system
- **Skeletal verdict: highly complex; many things can go wrong**

**Internet retail (Amazon model):**
- Goods: supplier → warehouse (often only after order placed) → direct to customer, skipping the expensive high street step
- Service quality: speed of delivery, website design, functionality, product range — inherently **more controllable and fixed**
- A website viewed in New York looks the same as in London or Hong Kong — quality is uniform
- As the firm grows, the same web infrastructure serves exponentially more customers
- **Skeletal verdict: simple, robust, scalable**

> "Whilst quality is inherently patchy at most high street retailers, it is fixed at Amazon." — Letter 12, June 2007

Sleep's conclusion: internet retailing has a far higher "power law" than high street equivalents — it can grow faster, become more profitable more quickly, and at larger scale without the skeletal complexity that eventually destroys traditional retailers.

### Amazon as the Investment Case
Nomad's investment in [[Amazon]] is justified partly by scaling law reasoning:
- **Simple skeletal structure** (internet model, warehouse-to-customer)
- **[[Scale_Economies_Shared]]** model: efficiency improvements passed to customers as lower prices, widening the moat as the firm grows
- **Founder orientation**: Jeff Bezos's "healthy disdain for Wall Street" allows long-horizon judgment calls
- **E-commerce runway**: US Census Bureau estimated e-commerce was 2.7–3.1% of all US retail sales in 2006. The question Sleep poses: what will it be in ten years?

> "When this basic building block is combined with the scale efficiencies shared model... customer centric orientation of the firm's founder... and his healthy disdain for Wall Street, this combination makes us think that we may have a mouse that can turn into an elephant." — Letter 12, June 2007

### Connection to Competitive Moat
The scaling laws framework reinforces [[Scale_Economies_Shared]]: a company with a high-power-law skeletal structure and the disposition to share scale benefits with customers sees its moat *widen* as it grows. Traditional competitors face increasing structural disadvantage as their own complexity grows. This is not a cyclical competitive edge but a structural one — the power law itself becomes a moat.
