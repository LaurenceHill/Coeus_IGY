# Cognitive Biases in Investing

## Summary
[[Nick_Sleep]] discusses cognitive biases in investing across multiple letters. Letter 5 covers attribution error and loss aversion asymmetry. Letter 7 addresses commitment bias — the risk that publicly discussing investments "forms mental chains" that prevent objective reassessment. Letter 8 introduces the brain's dual-system processing of immediate vs. deferred rewards (limbic override of analytical thinking). Letter 9 extends the framework with social proof (Milgram's crowd experiments) and the availability heuristic (Thaler's gorilla video). Together these form a coherent picture of the psychological hazards that separate good process from good-looking results. For the related Zeckhauser probability-based thinking framework, see [[Probability_Based_Thinking]].

## Explanation

### The Darwin Opening: Self-Deception is Universal

Sleep opens Letter 5 with Charles Darwin's account of his dissolute Cambridge years — only for a footnote by Darwin's son Francis to reveal that Darwin had "exaggerated the Bacchanalian nature of these parties." Sleep's takeaway: if Darwin, whose greatest intellectual gift was the ability *not* to fool himself, was capable of self-flattery in autobiography, investors are certainly not immune.

> "We all do that to some extent." — Letter 5, December 2003

### Bill Miller's Confession: Attribution Error

At the inaugural Legg Mason Investor Conference, Bill Miller admitted to analysing his **mistakes** more thoroughly than his **successes**. His reason: successes were automatically presumed to have risen *because of* the analysis. Sleep identifies this as a universal cognitive trap:

- **Winners**: flatter the ego; we attribute them to skill.
- **Losers**: feel bad; we over-investigate them.

The problem is that the wins may have risen for reasons entirely unrelated to the original thesis — luck, market sentiment, interest rate moves — while the analytical quality of the decision is left unexamined. The losers, meanwhile, may have involved excellent reasoning and simply been unlucky. Systematically examining only the losses teaches less than examining both.

### Loss Aversion Asymmetry

Sleep cites academic studies finding that **investment mistakes hurt up to three times more than gains satisfy** — a product of what he calls "primordial environmental conditioning designed to prevent us from repeatedly eating poisonous food." This is the loss aversion described by Kahneman and Tversky's Prospect Theory (though Sleep does not cite it by name).

The implication for investors is significant: because losses weigh heavier psychologically, investors tend to *over-correct* after mistakes and *under-learn* from wins. Disproportionate post-mortem attention on losses leads to risk-aversion that is asymmetric with the actual distribution of outcomes.

### The Stagecoach / Conseco Example

Sleep uses Nomad's own portfolio to make this concrete:

> "The Partnership's investment in [[Stagecoach_Group]] has in effect paid for the failure of [[Conseco]] six times over, and yet it was Conseco that we wrote to you about first." — Letter 5, December 2003

This illustrates the asymmetric salience of losses: the Conseco failure received immediate, detailed disclosure and a dedicated mea culpa in the September 2002 Global Investment Review. The Stagecoach success — which dwarfed the loss — received less prominent analytical attention, because successes feel self-evident.

### Sleep's Stated Goal: Honest Attribution

Sleep's aim in Letter 5 is explicitly to attempt Bill Miller's approach — to examine *why* the Partnership performed well, "whilst trying as best we can to avoid Darwin's error of fooling oneself." His conclusion: performance to date was primarily driven by "the decline in the level of fear felt by other investors" — market sentiment normalising from extremes of pessimism, not by uniquely brilliant stock selection. The stocks in Stagecoach, Xerox, and others rose as credit markets normalised and free cash flow became apparent, not because Sleep's specific insight was suddenly recognised.

This is a rare and valuable act of analytical honesty: attributing extraordinary returns primarily to macro-sentiment normalisation rather than to skill.

### Implications for the Investment Process

1. **Examine wins as rigorously as losses.** Was the thesis correct? Did the stock rise for the right reasons? Would you make the same decision again?
2. **Discount the psychological weight of losses.** A loss that was analytically sound is less damaging to process than a win that was analytically sloppy.
3. **Don't extrapolate.** The +79.6% in 2003 was partly sentiment-driven, not fully the product of replicable process. Mental extrapolation of that number into expectations for 2004 and beyond would be Darwin's error.

### Commitment Bias — Letter 7 (December 2004)
In the appendix to Letter 7, Sleep reproduces an essay from the September 2004 Global Investment Review on the risk of publicly discussing investments. Drawing on [[Charlie_Munger]]'s "bias from consistency and commitment tendency," he argues that fund managers who publicly commit to a position subtly and subconsciously lose their objectivity:

> "And of course, if you make public disclosure of your conclusions, you're pounding into your own head... what they are shouting out, they are pounding in." — Munger, Harvard Law School, c. 1995

The key danger: by trying to convince clients of an investment's merits, the manager may convince *himself* — forming "mental chains" that prevent objective reassessment when facts change. This is why Sleep is explicitly uncomfortable writing an essay about [[Costco_Wholesale]] at length: he knows that in doing so he risks impairing his own future judgment on the stock.

The practical implications: (1) Consultant meetings that "aggressively bear down on microeconomics of an individual business" are counterproductive — they form mental chains for both parties. (2) Monthly reporting "marks emotions to market," giving recent-poor-performance unwarranted salience. (3) The best answer at a cocktail party to "what's your largest holding?" may be to decline to answer — as one unnamed fund manager did.

Sleep also draws the inference that **fund management companies are either investment companies or marketing companies — not both**. The sales function (which works by "shouting out and pounding in") is structurally at odds with the objective thinking required for long-term performance.

### Immediate vs. Deferred Rewards — Letter 8 (June 2005)
Sleep cites neuroscience (McClure, Laibson, Loewenstein and Cohen, 2004): the brain processes immediate and deferred rewards in *different* brain regions. Immediate gains are processed by the limbic (survival) system, which can override the fronto-parietal (analytical) system. Stress induces this override — and money induces stress.

The consequence: **the more stressed investors are, the more they overweight short-term outcomes**. This is not irrational in an evolutionary sense (a meal today is more valuable than a feast next week if starvation is possible), but it is destructive in investing, where the deferred reward (compounding) is almost always the superior option.

Sleep uses the Punch cartoon of a crowd following a panicking figure to illustrate herding — and cites Robert Cialdini's *Influence: The Psychology of Persuasion* as the source. The rational response is to focus on the price-to-value ratio (the "birds in the bush") rather than recent price performance (the "bird in the hand").

### Three Competitive Advantages (Bill Miller Framework) — Letter 8
Sleep references Bill Miller's framework from a Columbia Business School speech, which identifies three sources of investment competitive advantage:
1. **Informational** — knowing a meaningful fact nobody else does
2. **Analytical** — cutting up public information to arrive at superior conclusions
3. **Psychological (behavioural)** — acting rationally when others cannot

Miller's (and Sleep's) conclusion: sustainable edge is almost always analytical and/or psychological, not informational. Information advantages decay quickly. Behavioural advantages — particularly the ability to hold through volatility without mental extrapolation — are durable.

> "The overwhelming advantage with regard to Nomad is the patience of the investor base and the alignment of that disposition with the analytical and psychological traits of your manager." — Letter 8, June 2005

### Social Proof — Letter 9 (December 2005)
Sleep draws on Stanley Milgram's street experiments: as the size of a crowd looking up at nothing increased, the proportion of passers-by who stopped and looked up rose in a non-linear, step-function manner. Once a critical mass was looking up, almost everyone stopped.

In markets: when enough investors pile into an idea, others follow not because the fundamentals have improved but because the crowd itself is the signal. Sleep connects this directly to the Thai cement overinvestment of the 1990s — once Siam Cement had built sufficient capacity, competitors followed purely out of fear of missing out. See [[Capital_Cycle]].

The [[Matichon]] takeover episode (September 2004) illustrates the same dynamic: institutional shareholders sold to the Thai PM-backed bidder collectively, each validating the others' decision, without any of them re-anchoring to the investment case. Sleep identified four simultaneous psychological errors: social proof, availability bias, principal-agent conflict, and poor probability-based thinking.

### Availability Heuristic — Letter 9 (December 2005)
Sleep cites Richard Thaler's gorilla-suit video experiment (the one where, when asked to count basketball passes by the white team, most observers fail to notice a man in a gorilla suit walk across the screen, beat his chest, and leave). The lesson: intense focus on one dimension makes people miss obvious facts in the same visual field.

Applied to investing: **the most important information is frequently not in the financial headlines**. What accountants expense — advertising, R&D, marketing — is valued at zero by the market. What auditors ignore entirely — product integrity, customer loyalty, management character — often drives the bulk of competitive advantage.

> "If it's in the headlines, it's in the price." — Letter 9 / speech appendix, December 2005

Sleep's example: when Estée Lauder announced increased advertising spend, its share price fell — the market treated all costs as bad costs. But advertising bought a brand asset that was simultaneously expensed on the income statement and ignored on the balance sheet. The availability heuristic led markets to anchor on reported earnings while missing the asset being built.

The availability heuristic and the social proof bias interact: vivid, salient, easily-available data (price has risen; peers are selling) overwhelms slower, harder-to-obtain structural analysis. The investor's task is to resist this and do the harder work. See [[Probability_Based_Thinking]] for the full decision-tree framework Sleep recommends as the antidote.

### Denial, Anchoring, and Drift — Letter 13 (December 2007)
Letter 13 extends the cognitive bias framework through the [[Mistakes_Framework]]. Three additional traps are identified:

**Denial**: reinventing reality to avoid facing a painful truth. The Bank X case study: after losing money on commercial property in the 1990s, the bank concluded it "could not do commercial property" — misclassifying the mistake as an asset-class problem rather than a credit-underwriting problem. The underlying cognitive error (poor judgment about credit risk) went unfixed, later causing equally bad losses in investment banking and international markets.

**Anchoring**: a static, historical view of a problem formed at the time of purchase, failing to evolve as facts change. Sleep's own [[Conseco]] mistake and [[Stagecoach_Group]] sale are both instances of anchoring — in opposite directions. Conseco: anchored to the original analysis, held through bankruptcy. Stagecoach: anchored to the original target price, sold before the full value was realised.

**Drift**: small incremental changes in thinking that build into a large mistake. The Buffett golf story (sleep cites Walter Schloss/Outstanding Investor Digest): offered a bet of win US&#36;10,000 for a hole-in-one but pay US&#36;10 on failure, Buffett declined — "If you are not disciplined in the little things, you won't be disciplined in the big things." The language corruption example: banks calling losses "market turbulence," companies calling revenue declines "negative growth." Once language drifts, perception of reality drifts too.

### Confirmation Bias / Mental Shortcuts — Letter 20 (June 2011)
Letter 20 opens with a vignette that Sleep uses to illustrate how minds close prematurely. Richard Zeckhauser passed a note to a fellow panelist at Harvard's JFK School of Government during an investment discussion:

> Zeckhauser: "One of our participants is buying Zimbabwean securities, which are priced at a small percentage of Net Asset Value. Would you try to invest with him?"
> Other panelist: "No!" (exclamation mark original)

Sleep's observation: the panelist made up his mind the moment he saw the word "Zimbabwe" — and that blocked all further thinking about the actual investment case (buying assets at a fraction of net asset value). [[Charlie_Munger]] at the same course captured the mechanism:

> "The human mind is a lot like the human egg: once one sperm has entered then all the other sperm are locked out." — Charlie Munger (cited Letter 20, June 2011)

A market research finding reinforces this: most car advertising is read *after* the purchase decision has already been made — and is read only to gather post-hoc rationalisation for a choice already fixed. The mind is not open; it is confirming.

Applied to investing: vivid negative associations (Zimbabwe, bankruptcy, scandal) trigger the same snap closure, preventing rational assessment of the actual odds. The correct antidote is Zeckhauser's probabilistic framework (see [[Probability_Based_Thinking]]) — making explicit the probability tree rather than allowing a label to substitute for analysis.

### Framing Error — Letter 18 (June 2010)
Sleep names a cognitive trap that had led him to under-value certain excellent businesses for years: the **framing error**. When analysing a firm's competitive advantage, the brain latches on to what is easily found and vivid — and stops looking:

> "I had gone looking for what I thought ought to be there, a vivid smoking gun such as a brand name, a location, a clever re-insurance contract, or a patent. However, there is no a priori reason why a comparative advantage should be one big thing, any more than many smaller things." — Letter 18, June 2010

The framing error led Sleep to dismiss the Welsh insurance company's "no secret sauce" answer as a fig leaf for the absence of genuine advantage. The retraction: "I think that all this time I may have been wrong." The interlocking, self-reinforcing network of small decisions is precisely what makes [[Scale_Economies_Shared]] businesses so hard to displace — and precisely what makes them hard to see. See [[Interlocking_Advantages]].

The framing error is structurally related to the **availability heuristic** (Letter 9): the vivid brand name is available; the thousand daily decisions by 147,000 employees are not. Both cause the same analytical failure — the mind anchors on what is salient rather than what is causally important.

### Truths Spoken Quietly — Letter 16 (June 2009)
Sleep opens Letter 16 with Charles Darwin's observation that when encountering ideas that contradicted his own, he found it necessary to write them down immediately — otherwise they would slip away. Sleep's application: the mind does not process threatening truths with the same salience as confirming ones.

He extends this with a generalization: truths tend to be spoken quietly, while shaky suppositions are shouted loudly. In markets, confident proclamation is often inversely correlated with analytical solidity. The analyst who shouts "this is the next Microsoft" is generally operating with far weaker foundations than the one who says "I think, on balance, this is a reasonable probability." The quiet portfolio (two-thirds of Nomad in companies that do not advertise) is the investment analogue of this: the best business propositions do not require loudness.
