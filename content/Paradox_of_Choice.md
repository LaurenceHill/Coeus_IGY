# Paradox of Choice

## Summary
[[Nick_Sleep]]'s application, in Letter 25 (December 2013), of psychologist Barry Schwartz's "Paradox of Choice" framework to the problem of investing. Schwartz observed that greater choice does not produce greater happiness — it produces greater expectation of perfection, and therefore greater disappointment when any specific choice falls short of an imagined optimum. Applied to markets, the implication is that the extraordinary number of available investments and the constant change in their prices makes it statistically near-certain that any portfolio will underperform some alternative at virtually any measurement date. The investor who understands this is liberated from the tyranny of relative comparison; the investor who does not is permanently dissatisfied.

## Explanation

### Schwartz's Original Observation
Barry Schwartz, in his 2004 book *The Paradox of Choice*, counted over 130 different salad dressings in his local supermarket (excluding olive oils and balsamic vinegars for home preparation), and over six million possible combinations of hi-fi components in an electronics store. Despite this proliferation of choice — ostensibly designed to satisfy every possible consumer preference — he could find no correlation with increased happiness. His hypothesis: the abundance of choice creates the (subconscious) expectation that the ideal choice exists and should be findable. This makes any actual choice disappointing by comparison with the imagined perfect option.

### The Stock Market Application
Sleep extends the observation to financial markets:

> "The public stock markets have many tens of thousands of potential investments, and the price of each of those changes almost constantly. The number of possible profit or loss combinations would make Schwartz's hi-fi store look like a multiple-choice test. It is very easy, therefore, to feel unhappy about one's investments. Indeed, on any one day, month, year it is highly likely, indeed statistically almost certain, that one's chosen combination of investments will lag alternatives — there will always be someone who did better." — Letter 25, December 2013

This is not a trivial observation. It means that even a genuinely excellent portfolio — one that will outperform significantly over a full market cycle — will routinely look inferior at any arbitrary measurement date. The quarterly benchmark comparison, the league table, the "have you seen what X is up?" conversation — all exploit this statistical inevitability to generate dissatisfaction with sound long-term positions.

### The Ferrari 250 GTO
Sleep illustrates the trap with a callback to a story first told in the June 2010 letter: a Ferrari 250 GTO bought by a collector in the 1960s for the effective price of £750, and still held today. A different 250 GTO had changed hands for US&#36;20m when first cited. By Letter 25, the update arrives: "Stop Press: a Ferrari 250 GTO changed hands recently for US&#36;52m! That represents an annual return of over twenty percent for around fifty years."

Nomad returned just over 20% per annum over its lifetime — comparable to a Ferrari 250 GTO. Yet:

> "There is always something going on that makes Nomad's performance look a bit weedy!"

This is Schwartz's paradox operating in real time: even a genuinely outstanding long-run return can be made to look inadequate by cherry-picking a comparison. The £750 Ferrari collector, by choosing to hold rather than compare, avoided the trap entirely.

### The Structural Defence: Long-Term Comparison Only
Sleep's response to the paradox is identical throughout the letters: "Whatever the yardstick, we ask only that Nomad be compared over the very long-term." Short-term comparison is structurally designed to produce dissatisfaction, because in the short term there is always a better alternative to point to. Long-term comparison is the only honest test of whether value was created.

This connects directly to [[Patience_as_Competitive_Advantage]] and [[Short_Termism]]: the investor who demands short-term relative comparison is, by the mathematics of the paradox, guaranteed to be dissatisfied. The investor who commits to a long time horizon exempts himself from the psychological damage.

### Connection to Portfolio Concentration
The paradox also supports [[Portfolio_Concentration]]: a concentrated portfolio in genuinely great businesses will underperform a broad index in any given period when those businesses lag — and will outperform decisively over long periods when the compounding operates. The diversified portfolio, by contrast, will never look dramatically bad (because it approximates the index) but will also never compound at the rates Sleep describes. Schwartz's paradox, ironically, is an argument for accepting the discomfort of short-term underperformance rather than diversifying it away.
