# Inactivity as Strategy

## Summary
[[Nick_Sleep]]'s argument, fully articulated in Letters 19–20 (December 2010 – June 2011), that the decision **not** to trade a portfolio is itself an active, rational decision — and perhaps the most underrated form of investment discipline. Inactivity requires resisting the "human itch to be seen to be doing something," the structural incentive of fee-based asset management, and the Pavlovian pull of short-term price moves. When applied to a portfolio of [[Character_as_Moat|great-character businesses]] undergoing compounding, inactivity is not laziness but the highest expression of long-term conviction.

## Explanation

### The Two Modes Restated
Sleep frames investment inactivity in the context of the two fundamental modes of investing:

1. **Buy cheap, sell at profit, repeat** — requires many decisions per year; the skill is in identifying mispricings; each transaction creates reinvestment risk.
2. **Buy a great business at a reasonable price and hold it indefinitely** — apparently requires one decision (buy), but "in reality it requires daily decisions not to sell the shares as well. Almost no one does this."

The second mode is rendered nearly impossible by industry incentives: "inactivity is the enemy of high fees." A fund manager who doesn't trade looks asleep at the wheel; the marketing department has nothing new to say; the client has nothing to point to as evidence the manager is working. All structural incentives push toward trading. Inactivity is therefore the harder discipline.

### The Active Non-Decision
Sleep coins the important clarification: the decision not to change the portfolio is an **active** decision, made daily, requiring continuous analytical upkeep:

> "Whilst a lack of buying and selling may look from the outside that we are not doing anything, the decision not to change the portfolio is an active decision and our research continues as ever." — Letter 19, December 2010

Sleep and Zakaria "find many great businesses available at what seem sensible prices, but, in our opinion, they do not compare favorably with what we already own." This is a form of continuous opportunity-cost analysis: every day, the existing portfolio is implicitly compared against the available universe and found to be the better option. Inactivity is the output of that continuous comparison, not the absence of analysis.

### The UK Top Gun Analogy
> "When I explained this to an old school friend of mine, who was trained to fly fast jets for the British Military, he said — 'Nick, it is really difficult to do nothing, after our training sometimes we would pilot transport planes and the instructors would make us physically sit on our hands to force us to think before doing anything'. If a UK Top Gun needs to sit on his hands, Zak and I may require straightjackets." — Letter 19, December 2010

The military analogy captures the discipline required: trained for action, the reflex is to act. The better pilot — and the better investor — sometimes recognises that the right move is no move.

### William James on Overlooking
Sleep quotes William James's formulation of the same insight:

> "The art of being wise is the art of knowing what to overlook." — William James (cited Letter 19, December 2010)

Applied to investing: the data stream passing across Sleep's desk — economic news, quarterly earnings, macro events, currency moves, political headlines — contains almost nothing that changes the relationship between Nomad's businesses and their customers. To be wise is to recognise which signals matter (structural changes to the customer-business bond) and which do not (almost everything reported in the financial press).

### The Mental Framework from Letter 21
Sleep later frames the "what matters" filter explicitly:

> "One trick that Zak and I use when sieving the data that passes over our desks is to ask the question: does any of this make a meaningful difference to the relationship our businesses have with their customers?" — Letter 21, December 2011

This filter, applied consistently, reduces the universe of "things to act on" to a small number of genuine signal events — and leaves inactivity as the rational default for everything else.

### Reinvestment Risk
Inactivity eliminates one of the most underappreciated costs in active fund management: **reinvestment risk** — the danger that capital returned from a sold position cannot be deployed at equally attractive terms.

> "If our firms can successfully grow, and we can resist the temptation to fiddle, then we can meaningfully reduce the reinvestment risk embedded in lots of share buying and selling." — Letter 17, December 2009

Every sale creates a cash position and an obligation to find the next idea. In a world where truly great businesses are scarce and chronically undervalued by the market (see [[Longevity_of_Compound]]), the probability of deploying proceeds at equal or better terms is structurally low. The better trade is often to hold the compounding machine and let it run.

### Pavlovian Antidote — Letter 19
Sleep is explicit about the psychological discipline required:

> "Please, please, please mentally take the excesses of the good years and apply them to the deficits of the poorer years. In our opinion, this mental trick will help you, and us, make much more rational decisions. This is because it helps to sidestep the Pavlovian response to a sharp change in prices and to sell apparent winners to buy losers or, indeed, the other way around." — Letter 19, December 2010

The "Pavlovian response" — the instinct to lock in gains after a strong year or cut losses after a weak one — is the enemy of inactivity. It is the mechanism by which good long-term positions are systematically exited at the wrong time by the investing public. Nomad's partners, by not redeeming in 2009, demonstrated the opposite disposition — and were explicitly thanked for it.

### "Don't Just Do Something, Stand There!" — Letter 23
Letter 23 opens with the White Rabbit's line from *Alice in Wonderland* — "Don't just do something, stand there!" — as a deliberate inversion of the usual imperative. Sleep and Zakaria describe their daily routine at "Galactic HQ": shuffling in, swapping trainers for slippers, and working through annual reports without executing a single purchase or sale transaction. "As far as purchase or sale transactions in Nomad are concerned, we are inactive. Inactive except, perhaps, for the observation, seldom made, that the decision not to do something is still an active decision; it is just that the accountants don't capture it."

The letter also introduces the real-world parallel: the [[Aggregation_of_Marginal_Gains]] in Zak's family activity centre and in Nomad's portfolio companies. Inactivity at the portfolio level is complemented by intense operational activity within the businesses themselves — Costco measuring costs in basis points, Amazon removing light bulbs from vending machines. The investor sits on hands; the businesses never stop improving.

### Portfolio Age and Runway — Letter 24
Letter 24 (June 2013) adds a structural dimension to the inactivity thesis. At the time of initial investment, Nomad's firms were on average **approximately fifteen years old** (twelve years, excluding the two "grandparents" Berkshire Hathaway and Costco). Sleep estimates the average S&P 500 constituent has been in the index for around twenty-five years, implying an age at inclusion of perhaps twenty years. Nomad's firms are therefore notably younger than the average large-cap — with considerably more runway ahead.

The implication for inactivity: holding young, high-quality, founder-run businesses is structurally different from holding mature or declining businesses. Inactivity with the former is not passivity — it is confidence in the length of the growth runway.

> "Our expectation is that this [inaction] is a considerable source of value added!" — Letter 24, June 2013

Sleep playfully promises partners a real-time update on Nomad's level of inaction through a planned "**Nomad Inactivity App.**" — available only in the Amazon App Store, of course.

Charlie Munger's formulation, cited in Letter 24, closes the argument neatly: **"You make your real money sitting on your assets."**
