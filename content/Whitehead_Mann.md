# Whitehead Mann

## Summary
Whitehead Mann Plc. is a UK executive search firm (headhunter to FTSE 100 CEOs) that became an [[Active_Ownership]] case study for [[Nomad_Investment_Partnership]] in Letter 11 (December 2006). When management — having declined to invest in the bankruptcy-rescuing placing — subsequently attempted to buy the entire company at a 5% premium to the rescue price, [[Nick_Sleep]] joined a dissenting shareholder block holding ~19% of shares outstanding: enough, under UK rules, to block compulsory acquisition.

## Explanation

### Business Background
Whitehead Mann is described as having "a reasonably entrenched position as the largest headhunter to FTSE 100 CEOs." This cyclically sensitive revenue stream was used to fund acquisitions of competitors — often with insiders cashing out in the process. When revenues declined, the interest burden overwhelmed cash flow, and the share price fell from a peak of £4 to approximately 50p.

### The Rescue Placing — and Management's Absence
When the company was near bankruptcy and seeking capital, it conducted a rights issue and placing at **40p per share**. Sleep and Zakaria attended meetings with management to assess whether to participate. Their key observation: **management was not buying shares alongside shareholders.** Their response:

> "We'll be bullish when you are bullish." — Sleep to the managing director (Letter 11)

Nomad declined to participate in the rescue placing. Other investors provided the capital at 40p that saved the company.

### The Insider Bid at 42p
After the rescue, management — alongside a private equity fund — offered to buy the entire company at **42p per share**, having secured irrevocable undertakings from approximately 70% of shareholders. The offer to shareholders who had just saved the company: a 2p gain (5%) on the rescue price.

Sleep's characterisation: management had chosen not to invest in the bankruptcy-avoiding placing, but now wished to use the "bulk purchasing facility" (the Scheme of Arrangement / takeover rules) to acquire the company for themselves. Sleep calls this "insider trading with the takeover rules being used as a bulk purchasing facility."

### Nomad's Response — Active Dissent
Nomad joined a dissenting shareholder group owning approximately **19% of shares outstanding**. Under UK rules, shareholders owning more than 10% cannot be compulsorily acquired — a protection not available in the United States. The 19% bloc therefore blocks the management buyout from becoming compulsory.

Sleep's interim valuation approach: Nomad will carry its now-unlisted Whitehead Mann shares at the takeover price (42p) plus (or minus) retained earnings. The view is that management — now with leveraged skin in the game — should be allowed to demonstrate what they can do without the scrutiny of the public markets.

### The Broader Pattern
Sleep uses the Whitehead Mann episode to reinforce the Weetabix lesson: the UK market is experiencing a wave of privatisations of small and mid-cap businesses at discounted prices. Institutional shareholders have abandoned these companies in favour of mega-caps, leaving shareholder bases dominated by insiders with minimal opposition.

> "In no other sphere of capitalism can your property be seized in exchange for cents on the dollar... But fund managers, who in their private capacity would be insulted if someone offered less than their house was worth, happily sell shares in their professional capacity at discounted prices to smart buyers." — Letter 11, December 2006

The solution Sleep identified in 2003 (Letter 5) and applies here: own enough of the company to block the bid. That requires concentration — exactly the opposite of the industry's diversification instinct. See [[Portfolio_Concentration]] and [[Active_Ownership]].
