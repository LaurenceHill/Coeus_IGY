# Character as Moat

## Summary
[[Nick_Sleep]]'s insight from Letter 17 (December 2009) that the character of a firm — its ability to resist the [[Locker_Room_Culture|locker room temptation]] to optimise for short-term appearances — is the deepest and most durable source of competitive advantage. Fewer than 5% of listed companies, in Sleep's estimate, do what they believe is right rather than what plays well with the outside world. Nomad is "overwhelmingly invested in businesses of great character." Character is also the mechanism by which reinvestment risk is materially reduced: a firm with a deeply embedded, customer-focused culture does not need to be replaced.

## Explanation

### The Insight
Sleep frames character explicitly as an investment variable:

> "We have learned or, rather, come to appreciate, that the character of a firm — call it the ability to resist locker room temptation — is far more important than first we realised. This is an important insight. In the long run it may be all that matters." — Letter 17, December 2009

The word "resist" is deliberate. The locker room pressure — to hit the quarterly earnings target, to tell the market what it wants to hear, to cut investment spending when cash is tight — is present at virtually every firm. Character is the capacity to absorb that pressure and maintain the long-run orientation anyway.

### Rarity
Sleep estimates that fewer than **5% of publicly listed firms** do what they think is right rather than what they think plays well with "media, Wall Street, investors." Yet the vast majority of Nomad's portfolio — despite being selected initially on valuation criteria — turns out to consist of such firms. Sleep attributes this to a "predilection": the same qualities that create great long-run businesses are the same qualities that create the kind of deliberate underearning (shared scale economies, investment in customer relationships, refusal to optimise for quarterly earnings) that makes these businesses cheap relative to their long-run value. The valuation screen and the character screen are, in practice, the same screen.

### The "Divine Discontent" Formula
The positive statement of character-as-moat comes from a private meeting Sleep had with one of Nomad's founder CEOs:

> "We start with the customer and work backwards… rather than set your standards by what others do, the firm benefited from a 'divine discontent' with the status quo, which kept colleagues on their toes and the firm improving, irrespective of what the competition were doing." — Nomad investee founder (cited Letter 17, December 2009)

The phrase **"divine discontent"** captures the internal compass of such firms: not competitor-referenced ("we must beat rival X") but customer-referenced ("are we doing the best we can for the customer?"). The competitive benchmark is constantly reset by the firm's own ideals, not by the industry average.

This directly connects to Jeff Bezos's formulation: "If you want to be successful, you have to be willing to be misunderstood." A company that does what plays well with analysts will be widely understood. A company that passes scale savings to customers, refuses to advertise, and invests at the expense of near-term margins will be widely misunderstood — and chronically undervalued. See [[Longevity_of_Compound]].

### Character and Reinvestment Risk
Sleep frames character as a risk-reduction tool specific to the second mode of investing (buy a great business and hold it indefinitely):

> "If our firms can successfully grow, and we can resist the temptation to fiddle, then we can meaningfully reduce the reinvestment risk embedded in lots of share buying and selling." — Letter 17, December 2009

**Reinvestment risk** — the danger that capital returned from a sold position cannot be deployed at equal or better terms — is the silent tax on active management. Every time a fund manager sells a position, they face the problem of finding something equally good to buy. A portfolio of firms with great character, held indefinitely, eliminates this problem. The high-character firm does the compounding; the investor does nothing except wait.

### Character and Business Culture
Letter 18 adds the structural dimension: cultures that care about "the little things" are extraordinarily difficult to build and almost impossible to retrofit:

> "In the opinion of Amazon.com founder Jeff Bezos, [a culture of caring about little things is] almost impossible to create if not put in place at the firm's genesis." — Letter 18, June 2010

The Sol Price 1967 memo (see [[Interlocking_Advantages]]) is Sleep's best exhibit: 43 years before Letter 18, Sol Price wrote the founding document of what would become Costco's culture. Forty-three years later, the culture was intact. The firm's advantage is not patent-protected, not legally defensible — it is simply the accumulated character of thousands of employees making thousands of decisions about what is right.

This is why [[Scale_Economies_Shared]] businesses with strong founding cultures are so durable: the moat is not one defensible asset but a million interlocking daily decisions all pointing in the same direction.

### The Real Risks Are Not in the Newspapers — Letter 21 (December 2011)
Sleep argues that the existential risks to great-character businesses are not the macro events dominating financial headlines (credit crises, currency crises, budget crises) but the internal failures of character that could break the bond with customers:

> "It won't be the on-line fraud, per se, or, heaven forbid, an airplane crash at AirAsia, that could ruin our firms, but a disingenuous response to a problem." — Letter 21, December 2011

The positive case: when a firm comes clean after a problem, explains it candidly, learns from it, and makes customers whole, "the long-term relationship with customers has probably not been harmed too much and, if done properly, may even have been enhanced." When Sleep asked the founder of AirAsia what he would do if his firm put a plane in a swamp, the founder raised both hands in a gesture of having nothing to hide — "that response strikes us as very healthy."

The negative case: News International's response to the phone-hacking scandal. Making light of a serious situation, pretending it was minor when it was not, risked breaking "even a long-standing bond of trust" — and ultimately did.

The analytical implication: traditional risk frameworks focus on the wrong things. Macro events and operational accidents are recoverable; dishonest responses to problems are not. A character-based portfolio is therefore de-risked against the most common form of corporate destruction — not the accident, but the cover-up.

### The Investment Implication
Character screens are harder to quantify than financial screens. This is precisely why they are underweighted by the market. The fund manager with a quarterly reporting cycle cannot put "character of management team" on a discounted cash flow model. Sleep and Zakaria can hold for a decade, long enough to observe whether the culture survives competitive pressure, a recession, and management succession.

The portfolio composition — "overwhelmingly invested in businesses of great character" — is not a coincidence. It is the output of a multi-year filtering process in which companies that blinked (cut investment, raised prices, gamed earnings) were sold, and companies that maintained their orientation through adversity were held.
