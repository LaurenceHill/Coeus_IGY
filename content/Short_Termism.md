# Short-Termism

## Summary
Short-termism is [[Nick_Sleep]]'s term for the dominant dysfunction in professional investment markets: the structural compression of time horizons driven by career risk, quarterly reporting cycles, and benchmark-relative measurement. Sleep views short-termism as both a pathology to understand and an exploitable inefficiency — it creates the mispricings that make [[Nomad_Investment_Partnership]] possible.

## Explanation

### The Structural Cause
Short-termism is not primarily a failure of intellect but of incentives. Professional fund managers face career risk if they underperform over any observable period. This incentivises them to:
- Track the index closely (avoiding "wrong" positions)
- Sell anything that is flat or falling this quarter
- Buy anything rising this quarter (momentum)
- Ignore multi-year investment theses that won't "work" within the current reporting period

Sleep identifies this as the **dominant dynamic** in markets during the early 2000s. The average holding period implied by Xerox's trading volume was under four months — far too short for any investor to be thinking about the five-to-ten-year earnings trajectory.

### The 100%-Certain-Stock Thought Experiment
In Letter 2 (June 2002), Sleep uses a thought experiment to illustrate the dysfunction vividly:

> "Imagine that you knew with 100% certainty of outcome, that on January 1st next year a company would come by some good fortune…which would result in the price of the share quickly rising tenfold. You and I would buy the shares today and wait. However, to the short-term investor the utility of this piece of information would be nought until after this year is ended."

The short-term investor cannot act on this information because a flat stock for 11 months creates career risk within the current year. This means even **certain value** can remain unrecognised for extended periods — which is precisely what allows Nomad to buy at a discount.

### Momentum Investing as a Consequence
Sleep explicitly connects short-termism to momentum investing: when managers are forced to hold only what is working this quarter, expensive shares become more expensive (buying pressure) and cheap shares become cheaper (selling pressure). The mechanism amplifies mispricings in both directions.

> "This is momentum investing and is the mechanism by which expensive shares become very expensive, just as cheap shares may become very cheap." — Letter 2, June 2002

### The Estée Lauder Example
Letter 2 gives a live illustration: Estée Lauder announced it would invest in brand building, reducing near-term earnings. The stock fell 15% in a day. Sleep's view: the company was **punished for being sensible**. Short-term investors responded to the next quarter's profit warning without analysing the long-term effect on brand value and pricing power.

> "In effect the company had been punished by the markets for being sensible. It is this behaviour that gets us excited and sets up investment opportunities." — Letter 2, June 2002

### The Fred Schwed Prescription
Letter 2 quotes Fred Schwed's *Where Are the Customers' Yachts?* for the antidote:

> "When there is a stock-market boom…sell…buy back the stocks…Continue to repeat this operation as long as you live, and you'll have the pleasure of dying rich." The operative phrase: "**pay no attention**."

For short-term investors, "pay no attention" is professionally impossible. For Nomad, it is the entire model.

### How Nomad Exploits Short-Termism
Short-termism creates the conditions for the [[Margin_of_Safety]]. A stock trading at 50 cents on the dollar is only possible if the majority of holders have no interest in the long-run value. Nomad's competitive advantage is therefore the combination of:
1. Having the analytical skill to identify intrinsic value.
2. Having investors with long enough time horizons to allow the thesis to play out.

See [[Patience_as_Competitive_Advantage]].

### The Collapse of Holding Periods — Letter 14 (June 2008)
Sleep cites data from the Bogle Financial Markets Research Center to chart the structural compression of holding periods:

| Era | Average holding period |
|---|---|
| 1950s | ~7 years |
| 2006 | <1 year |

In the same period, the annualised turnover rate of mutual funds rose from ~16% to ~100%. Sleep's framing: "in 1950 the average share was held for around seven years; by 2006 for less than one year." For Nomad's specific stocks, the picture was even more extreme: analysis of share register turnover implied that the average holder of Nomad's stocks held them for approximately **51 days**.

> "The average shareholder of our firms holds their stake for a mere 51 days! ... We are not sure what value is added by those who trade in and out of our portfolio companies with such haste." — Letter 14, June 2008

### Momentum as Giffen Good — Letter 14 (June 2008)
By mid-2008, Empirical Research Partners described the market as "the greatest momentum market since the dot-com era." Sleep uses the economic concept of a **Giffen good** — a product that becomes *more* demanded as its price rises, inverting normal price-demand relationships — to describe momentum stocks. Potash Corp. is cited: its share price rose 400% in a year as farmers demanded fertiliser regardless of price because crop prices had also risen. When the underlying driver (food prices) reverses, the cascade unwinds violently.

The Giffen-good nature of momentum means the bubble is self-validating on the way up: rising prices attract more buyers, which raises prices, which attracts more buyers. Until it doesn't.

> "When prices stop rising and then fall, this very same mechanism reverses... What began as a self-reinforcing mechanism upward, then reinforces downward." — Letter 14, June 2008

### Keynes and "Managers' Capitalism" — Letter 14
Sleep quotes John Maynard Keynes's observation that markets had undergone "a pathological mutation from the system capitalism towards another, called managers' capitalism." The original capitalist was a risk-taking owner who thought in decades. The fund manager-shareholder thinks in quarters. When managers' capitalism dominates, the time-horizon of the whole system contracts — with predictable consequences for resource allocation and corporate behaviour.

This connects to John Bogle's argument (cited in Letter 14): mutual fund managers now earn fees on AUM, not on absolute returns. Their incentive is AUM growth — which requires marketing, which requires short-term performance — not client wealth creation.

### The Bubble Act Analogy — Letter 14
Sleep cites Charlie Munger at the 2008 Wesco Financial AGM on the Bubble Act: Parliament banned the issuance of new shares from 1720 to 1825 — over a century — as a response to the South Sea Bubble. Munger's provocative thesis: this forced all economic activity into partnership structures (unlimited liability), which may have been a necessary condition for the sustained investment in infrastructure (canals, mills, factories) that underpinned the British Industrial Revolution. The argument is that concentrated, long-term ownership — enforced by structure — produced better outcomes than dispersed, liquid shareholding.

The irony Sleep draws: today's capital markets, with their 51-day average holding periods, are structurally closer to the frenzied South Sea Bubble than to the patient partnership model that the Bubble Act accidentally enforced.

### Schlock Begets Schlock — Letter 17 (December 2009)
Letter 17 identifies a self-reinforcing feedback loop between the short-termism of fund managers and the short-termism of the companies they own:

> "We would argue that not only do companies get the investors they deserve, but investors also get the companies they deserve. Schlock begets schlock, as it were." — Letter 17, December 2009

The mechanism: mutual funds seeking short-term performance pressure companies for quarterly earnings. Companies, in turn, sacrifice long-term investment to hit those targets. Both parties deny this dynamic to their clients. Sleep cites Upton Sinclair: "it is difficult to get a man to understand something when his salary (or bonus) depends on his not understanding it."

The implication is that short-termism is not just a symptom of mis-incentivised managers but a systemic property — it propagates from investor to company and back. The antidote is not a rule or a regulation but a different kind of investor. See [[Locker_Room_Culture]] and [[Character_as_Moat]].

### The Preferred Remedy: Tax Short-Term Investing — Letter 25
Letter 25 contains Sleep's most direct policy prescription on short-termism. Critiquing the explosion of blanket financial regulation that treats all funds identically regardless of holding period or complexity, he offers an alternative — deliberately delivered as a whisper:

> "And if you really want to change industry behaviour, I'll whisper this, **tax short term investing**." — Letter 25, December 2013

The logic is simple: blanket rules penalise honest, long-term operators while sophisticated bad actors adapt and find workarounds. A turnover tax on short-term investing would instead target the *behaviour* at the root of most market dysfunction — directly discouraging the momentum trading, quarterly earnings gaming, and principal-agent exploitation that blanket regulation fails to address. Long-term investors would be automatically exempted. See [[Regulation_and_Industry_Structure]].

### The Drachten Roundabout — Letter 14
Sleep uses Hans Monderman's Drachten traffic experiment as an analogy for good incentive design. Monderman removed all traffic lights, road markings, and signs from a busy Dutch intersection. Result: accidents fell dramatically. The removal of rules forced drivers to make eye contact and exercise individual judgment — shifting responsibility from the system to the participant. The opposite of the Coventry "no exit" design (maximum rules, minimum thought, perverse outcomes).

Applied to investing: over-reliance on rules (quarterly reporting, benchmarks, short-term incentives) creates "busy incompetence" — managers follow the rules and destroy value. True alignment comes from removing the perverse rules and creating genuine skin in the game. See [[Slack]].
