# Diworsification

## Summary
"Diworsification" is Peter Lynch's term — adopted and used by [[Nick_Sleep]] — for the value-destroying form of corporate diversification. It occurs when management, having exhausted good reinvestment opportunities in their core business, allocate capital into unrelated or inferior businesses. The result is a decline in aggregate returns on capital and an accompanying fall in share price. It is Sleep's primary framework for understanding why even great franchise businesses underperform over time.

## Explanation

### The Concept
Sleep introduces diworsification in Letter 4 (June 2003) as the most common failure mode for businesses with [[Per_Share_Discipline]] or [[Franchise_Businesses|franchise]] economics:

> "All too often however management become sidetracked and misallocate capital — usually through diversification or, in the words of Peter Lynch, 'diworsification'. The result of which is that aggregate returns on capital decline and the share price falls to discount poor performance." — Letter 4, June 2003

The key word is "aggregate": a franchise business with a 20% return on capital does not suddenly earn 20% on the diversified sum if the new businesses earn 8%. The average is dragged down. The market correctly re-rates the company to reflect the blended (lower) returns.

### Why It Happens
Sleep's diagnosis ties directly to management character and incentives:
- Management of a mature franchise have less to do — the core business is running well.
- Growth-oriented incentives (EPS targets, acquisition bonuses, empire-building psychology) push them toward deploying capital elsewhere.
- Investment bankers actively encourage this — they earn fees on deals, not on doing nothing.
- The market rewards apparent revenue growth in the short term, masking the per-share value destruction.

This is why [[Per_Share_Discipline]] is so valuable as a screen: a company that has not issued shares in a decade has probably not made the empire-building acquisitions that diworsify returns.

### The [[Charlie_Munger]] "Cancer Surgery" Connection
Diworsification and the [[Charlie_Munger|cancer surgery approach]] are mirror images. Diworsification is the disease: diversification accumulates weak subsidiaries around a franchise core, diluting returns. Cancer surgery is the cure: removing the weak businesses to reveal the jewel. Sleep's investment in [[Stagecoach_Group]] is the most direct application — the Coach USA acquisition was textbook diworsification; Souter's return was cancer surgery in action.

### The [[Weetabix_Limited]] Contrast
Weetabix is the positive case: the George family resisted all offers and temptations to diversify for 70 years. The company remained focused on breakfast cereals, compounded its brand and market share slowly and steadily, and arrived in 2003 as the UK's largest-selling breakfast cereal with an unchanged share count since the 1930s. No diworsification. Sleep: "Value creation is often most sustainable when it is built slowly."

### Examples of Diworsification in the Nomad Letters
| Company | Core Franchise | Diworsifying Move | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| [[Stagecoach_Group]] | UK bus operations | Debt-funded acquisition of Coach USA (LBO roll-up) | Shares fell from £2.85 to 10p |
| [[Xerox_Corporation]] | High-end printer manufacturing | Earnings manipulation to hit 15% EPS growth target | SEC investigation, near-bankruptcy |
| Coca-Cola (1980s, [[Charlie_Munger]] example) | Syrup manufacturing and marketing | Shrimp farms, winery, film studio, bottling plants | Re-rated after asset sales; 10× over decade |

### The "What Are Returns on Incremental Capital?" Test
Sleep describes the analytical question that protects against diworsification:

> "We work hard reading annual reports…trying to answer the questions: what are returns on incremental capital and the longevity of those returns, are management correctly incented to allocate capital appropriately?" — Letter 4, June 2003

If incremental capital is being deployed at returns below the cost of capital or below the core business's returns, diworsification is already happening — whether management call it "diversification" or "strategic investment."

### The Concentrated Wealth Argument — Letter 16 (June 2009)
Letter 16 delivers the sharpest possible refutation of diversification-as-safety by surveying the world's greatest wealth builders:

> "Sam Walton had, at various times, the majority of his wealth in Wal-Mart. So did Bill Gates in Microsoft, Andrew Carnegie in Carnegie Steel, John D Rockefeller in Standard Oil, Carlos Slim in Telmex and Li Ka-shing in the Hong Kong Electric Company. And Warren Buffett has nearly all of his wealth in Berkshire Hathaway." — Letter 16, June 2009

All of history's greatest fortunes were built through radical concentration, not diversification. Sleep's inference: diversification is not the path to exceptional outcomes. It is, at best, insurance against disaster — and at worst, a device that guarantees mediocrity.

The corollary: "real" diversification is available cheaply via index funds. If a fund manager is running a 200-stock portfolio, he is providing poor-man's index tracking at active-management fees. The honest answer is that fund managers have "so few insights" rather than "so many" — and the diversified portfolio is a confession of that, disguised as prudence.
