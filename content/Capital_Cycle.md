# Capital Cycle

## Summary
The capital cycle framework is [[Nick_Sleep]]'s lens for understanding how high asset valuations attract excess supply, which eventually destroys the economics discounted in stock prices. First named explicitly in Letter 10 (June 2006) in the context of commodity stocks, it explains why industries that appear to be "New Era" opportunities typically end in overinvestment and collapse — and why the patience to wait for the anti-bubble is rewarded. Sleep uses Siam Cement/Thailand and Phelps Dodge as the anchor cases.

## Explanation

### The Core Mechanism
> "In a traditional capital cycle framework, the high valuation of these businesses encourages a supply side response, which eventually undermines the economics discounted in the stock price. The operative word here is 'eventually'." — Letter 10, June 2006

When an industry earns returns above its cost of capital, capital flows in. New entrants build capacity, existing players expand, and management teams — incentivised to grow — accelerate spending. The excess capacity eventually drives prices down until returns fall to or below the cost of capital. By the time this is apparent in reported results, the damage is done.

### The Thai Cement Precedent
Sleep uses the Thai cement industry in the mid-1990s as the canonical case:

Siam Cement built all the capacity Thailand could need. There was no rational need for Siam City Cement, TPI Polene, or others to join in — but they all did, through **fear of missing out** (social proof + envy + financial incentives). The result was catastrophic overinvestment, followed by the 1997 Asian financial crisis, followed by cement stocks trading at tiny fractions of replacement cost. This is where [[Deep_Discount_to_Replacement_Cost]] opportunities are born.

> "Once Siam Cement had built all the capacity Thailand could need there was no need for Siam City Cement to join in. Let alone TPI Polene. But they all went mad." — Letter 9, December 2005

The day Holderbank bought control of Siam City Cement from the family at Tb170/share (replacement cost of assets), the stock closed at Tb48 — less than one-third of the trade price. The stock subsequently rose ten-fold from that level.

### Commodity Stocks (Letter 10, June 2006)
By mid-2006, Sleep identified a capital cycle developing in commodity stocks. The indicators:
- Phelps Dodge (copper miner) had an average investor holding period of **three months** — inconsistent with the multi-decade asset life of a mining company
- Oil services companies were valued 3-4× more highly than their customers (echoing Cisco's premium to its customers in 1999)
- Capital spending plans were expected to increase **~40%** vs 2005
- Management teams had moved from "blocking and tackling" to a "glamorous receiver" mentality

Sleep's interpretation: "It is almost as if investors know it's a lie." A stock price that embeds a multi-decade New Era prediction, yet whose average shareholder changes every three months, is internally contradictory. The short average holding period implies investors do not actually believe the long-term story embedded in the price.

> "Human nature being as it is, we can almost guarantee that capital spending will continue to grow until share prices decline." — Letter 10, June 2006

### The Anti-Bubble
Capital cycle analysis cuts both ways. When capital pours into one area, it typically drains from another. Sleep uses this to identify investment opportunity:

> "When there is a frenzy of activity in one area of the market there is very often an anti-bubble of discarded companies." — Letter 10, June 2006

- In the dot-com era, the anti-bubble was companies with steady cash flow (ignored for being "boring")
- In 2006, the anti-bubble was **large high-quality growth businesses** — discarded in favour of commodity stocks, appearing cheaper relative to their history than they had in years

Sleep observes a structural shift in Nomad's portfolio: whereas five years earlier the cheapest stocks were the "detritus of the New Era boom" (distressed, beaten-up names like Stagecoach, Xerox, Lucent), by 2006 the discount to fair value was narrower but the dollar size of individual opportunities was larger. This made the case for reopening Nomad worth considering.

### Capital Cycle and Position Sizing
For commodity stocks or capital-cycle-driven opportunities to qualify as Nomad investments, Sleep requires confidence that sector economics are **not discounted several years out** in prices. Given rising capex plans in 2006 commodities, that was not achievable — so Sleep did not buy despite attractive apparent short-term economics.

This is an important distinction from the [[Deep_Discount_to_Replacement_Cost]] model: that model works when capex has *already been suppressed* for years and replacement cost is distant from current price (Zimbabwe, Asian crisis). The capital cycle model says: avoid entering when everyone is rushing to add capacity, because that capacity will compress returns.

### Relationship to Social Proof
The capital cycle is driven partly by psychology — specifically [[Probability_Based_Thinking|social proof]]: once one company starts building capacity, others follow for fear of missing out. This is the feedback mechanism that turns rational initial investment into irrational industry-wide overinvestment. The cure is probability-based thinking: would rational assessment of the full decision tree support this level of industry spending?
