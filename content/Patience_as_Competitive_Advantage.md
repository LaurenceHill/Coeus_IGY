# Patience as Competitive Advantage

## Summary
[[Nick_Sleep]] argues that patience is not merely a virtue for investors — it is an **edge**. Specifically, the aggregate patience of the [[Nomad_Investment_Partnership]]'s investor base is one of the fund's two stated competitive advantages (alongside the managers' capital allocation skill). This framing elevates the investor-manager relationship from transactional to strategic.

## Explanation

### The Core Argument
Because most professional investors are structurally short-term (see [[Short_Termism]]), a manager with patient capital can hold positions for multiple years while short-termists sell, creating and then closing mispricings. But this only works if investors don't redeem when the thesis takes time to play out. A manager with impatient investors has no real long-term edge, because redemption pressure forces the same short-term behaviour the manager is trying to exploit.

Sleep makes this explicit across both early letters:

> "If Nomad is to have a competitive advantage over our peers this will come from the capital allocation skills of your manager (if any) and the patience of our investors. Only by looking further out than the short-term crowd can we expect to beat them." — Letter 2, June 2002

> "One of Nomad's key advantages will be the aggregate patience of its investor base." — Letter 1, December 2001

### The "Partnership" Name as Signal
Sleep directly connects the word "Partnership" in the fund's name to this concept:

> "It is for this reason we named Nomad an Investment Partnership and not a fund. The relationship we seek is quite different." — Letter 2, June 2002

The name signals to prospective investors what is expected of them: a long-term commitment, not a trading account. This framing also creates a self-selection mechanism — investors who don't understand or accept this framing are unlikely to invest, which improves the average patience of the investor base.

### Validation in Practice
By December 2002, after more than a year of volatile and often negative absolute returns, Sleep notes:

> "In the latter we have started well, with no investor turnover since we began and almost no enquiries into performance despite the general decline in market prices. This is very unusual and a huge credit to our investors." — Letter 3, December 2002

This real-world data point confirms that the signalling worked: investors understood and accepted the long-term framework.

### Why Patience Has Optionality Value
Patience is not just about not selling — it also has an **offensive** dimension. When short-term investors are most panicked (e.g., autumn 2002), patient managers can **add** to positions or initiate new ones. Sleep did exactly this, reducing cash from ~20% in summer 2002 to ~5% by December 2002, with new purchases made at what he believed were maximum investor distress levels.

### The Tension with Performance Fees
Sleep acknowledges a tension: "holding cash does not meet our long-term investment goals (to say little of earning our incentive fees)." If positions are flat for extended periods, no performance fee accrues. This creates a soft pressure toward action, which Sleep resists. The answer is clear: the incentive fee must be a reward for long-term value creation, not a pressure toward short-term activity.

### "Never Mark Emotions to Market"
Letter 4 (June 2003) introduces one of Sleep's sharpest aphorisms on patience:

> "A stoical disposition to short-term results is both the right way to think (never mark emotions to market) but it also prepares one for results that may be reasonable but are unlikely to be an extrapolation of the last two years." — Letter 4, June 2003

"Mark emotions to market" is a deliberate play on the accounting term "mark to market" (pricing assets at current market value). Sleep's warning: just as continuously re-marking a portfolio to daily prices creates noise that obscures long-run value, continuously re-marking one's *emotional state* to recent performance creates the same dysfunction. A 40% gain in 18 months should not produce euphoria any more than a -2.6% six-month period should produce despair. The underlying businesses determine the outcome; the sequence of annual returns is largely irrelevant.

### Preferred Measurement Period: Five Years
Letter 4 also clarifies Sleep's preferred time frame for judging performance:

> "Our preference is for results to be measured over a five-year time frame, and even this may be a little short compared to the average holding period of the underlying investments which is presently around ten years." — Letter 4, June 2003

This framing is a direct challenge to quarterly and annual reporting norms. Sleep is telling Partners explicitly: if you are judging us on anything shorter than five years, you are using the wrong ruler.

### Selectivity on New Investors
Letter 4 introduces a dimension of patience applied *inward* — to fund growth itself:

> "We have only one chance to grow Nomad to a reasonable size before it is closed, and the quality of investors when the doors are shut is of great importance to us. To date, we have declined almost as many investment dollars as we have let in." — Letter 4, June 2003

Sleep is willing to forego AUM growth to protect the patience of the investor base. Accepting fast money or performance-chasing capital would undermine the very edge the fund depends on.

### Bill Miller's Three Competitive Advantages — Letter 8 (June 2005)
Sleep explicitly maps Nomad's edge onto the framework from Bill Miller's Columbia Business School speech:

1. **Informational edge** — knowing a meaningful fact nobody else does (rarely sustainable)
2. **Analytical edge** — processing public information to arrive at superior conclusions
3. **Psychological/behavioural edge** — acting rationally when others cannot (most durable)

Sleep's conclusion: "The overwhelming advantage with regard to Nomad is the patience of the investor base and the alignment of that disposition with the analytical and psychological traits of your manager. It simply would not work otherwise."

Miller's encapsulation of the investment task: **"exploit the fact that fewer things will happen than can happen."** Rather than trying to price all possible outcomes, focus on the high-probability central case and stay there while the market churns through its full probability tree. Costco's predictability — its future made more knowable by the fixity of its model — is why it is Sleep's largest holding. Its "cone of uncertainty" has a much narrower radius than most businesses.
