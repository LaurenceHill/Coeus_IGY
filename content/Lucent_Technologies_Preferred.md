# Lucent Technologies — 8% Redeemable Convertible Preferred

## Summary
Lucent Technologies' 8% Redeemable Convertible Preferred Stock was a fixed-income investment made by [[Nomad_Investment_Partnership]] in autumn 2002. It illustrates Sleep's willingness to invest across the capital structure — purchasing preferred stock rather than equity when the risk/reward was superior — and his frustration with management who made the wrong capital allocation choice at the bottom of the cycle.

## Explanation

### The Security
Lucent's 8% Redeemable Convertible Preferred stock was an unusual instrument:
- Purchased at **US&#36;350 per share** (par = US&#36;1,000/share).
- Could be **"put back"** to Lucent at par in August 2004, for payment in cash or a variable number of common shares.
- Cumulative dividends of 8% per annum meant that before the put date, pref holders would receive **almost half their investment back in dividends alone**.

### The Investment Logic
At the time of purchase, Lucent's common equity market cap was very low. If the share price failed to rise, the put would require delivering ~half of all common shares to pref holders — massively dilutive to existing common equity holders. This meant:

1. The put made the preferred stock the **first liability** management had to deal with — top of the to-do list.
2. Half the investment would be recovered through dividends **before** the put came due.
3. Lucent had enough **cash on deposit to redeem the prefs and all outstanding debt entirely** — the market was simply too pessimistic to notice.

> "In effect, one half of Lucent's pro-forma common equity could be purchased for US&#36;500m (the market value of the prefs) — a figure equivalent to only a few months' research and development spending." — Letter 4, June 2003

### The Management Decision: A Case Study in Poor Capital Allocation
Sleep and [[Marathon_Asset_Management]] urged management to take option **(c)**: buy back the prefs and common shares at distressed prices, repay debt with cash on hand, and return the firm to an unlevered balance sheet. This would have preserved value for common shareholders.

Management had three choices:
- **(a)** Continue operating, hoping for cash flow improvement.
- **(b)** Dilute existing shareholders (already down 98% from peak) by issuing more equity to redeem the prefs.
- **(c)** Use cash on hand to repurchase prefs and shares — preserve common equity.

Management chose **(b)** — what Sleep calls the "sleep-at-night choice" (keeping a cash cushion by issuing equity rather than deploying the cash they already had).

> "Management marked their emotions and the company's balance sheet to market at the weakest point in the cycle." — Letter 4, June 2003

The Woody Allen quote Sleep uses to characterise the choice: *"More than any time in history mankind is at a crossroads. One path leads to despair and utter hopelessness, the other to total extinction. Let us pray we have the wisdom to choose correctly."* — the implication being that management saw only two bad options and missed the obviously correct third.

### Outcome
> ⚠️ **Snapshot — Letter 4 (June 2003).** The preferred stock had tripled by the time of this letter and Sleep stated it would be sold as new investments become available. This is a closed/exiting position as of Letter 4.

Management chose option (b). The new common shares provided resources to meet the put, which was now covered many times over with cash. The preferred stock rose **threefold** to trade at a premium to par, as the 8% yield attracted fixed income investors once solvency concerns faded. Sleep notes the investment reached full value and would be sold as new opportunities arose.

### Key Lessons
1. **Cross-capital-structure investing**: Preferred stock can be the superior instrument when it sits at the top of the payment queue and delivers most of its value through contracted cash flows.
2. **Management quality matters at the margin**: A rational management team (option c) would have created far more value for common shareholders. Sleep's frustration here is a live demonstration of why [[Owner_Oriented_Management]] is non-negotiable.
3. **Market pessimism creates asymmetric opportunity**: The capital markets had "all but given up on the company" even though the cash to solve the problem was already on the balance sheet.
