# Dilution Risk

## Summary
Dilution risk is [[Nick_Sleep]]'s term for the danger that a company which appears cheap will destroy per-share value by issuing equity at a price below intrinsic worth — potentially turning what appeared to be a 50-cent dollar purchase into a purchase above intrinsic value. Articulated in Letter 14 (June 2008) as the primary reason for selling [[Nomad_Investment_Partnership]]'s position in MBIA (the Municipal Bond Insurance Association), it is one of the most important risks for deep-value investors who invest in financially stressed companies.

## Explanation

### The Mechanics
Sleep illustrates dilution risk with a mathematical example:

| Step | Assumption |
|---|---|
| Fair value of firm | US&#36;2bn |
| Shares outstanding | 60m |
| Intrinsic value per share | ~US&#36;33 |
| Price paid by value investor | US&#36;15 (47c on the dollar) |
| Capital raised at US&#36;10 (30m new shares) | US&#36;300m to fund US&#36;300m in losses |
| Intrinsic value per share after raising | ~US&#36;22 (was US&#36;28 before losses) |
| Effective price paid (now appears to be) | US&#36;15 / US&#36;22 = **68c on the dollar** |

The situation worsens dramatically as the share price falls further:

| Capital raise price | Intrinsic value after raising | Effective cost basis |
|---|---|---|
| US&#36;10/share | ~US&#36;22 | 68c on the dollar |
| US&#36;5/share | ~US&#36;16 | 94c on the dollar |
| US&#36;1/share | ~US&#36;5.50 | **3× intrinsic value** |

> "In the final example, original purchases made at U&#36;15, at what was thought to be less than half value, end up proving to have been made at three times intrinsic value!" — Letter 14, June 2008

### Why It Accelerates
Dilution risk is self-reinforcing: as share prices fall further, the same dollar amount of new equity requires issuing more shares at lower prices, which causes intrinsic value per share to fall further, which requires more capital, and so on. The investor who bought at US&#36;15 believing they had bought a bargain has no way of knowing the final per-share value without knowing (a) the total size of eventual losses and (b) the price of any future capital raise. Both are unknowable in real time.

### MBIA: The Live Example
MBIA (Municipal Bond Insurance Association), the world's largest bond insurer, diversified from municipal bonds into insuring securitised mortgage-backed securities — and suffered catastrophic losses when the underlying mortgages defaulted. When Nomad purchased MBIA:
- Shares had already taken a sizeable write-down
- Capital had been raised twice
- New management had been installed and were buying shares themselves
- The market implied the shares were at a meaningful discount to anything but the worst-case scenario

But the outstanding question — how large were the eventual credit losses? — ranged from US&#36;2bn (company estimate) to US&#36;6–8bn (sceptics) to US&#36;13.6bn (Moody's worst case) to greater still (implied by the share price discount to claims-paying resources).

Sleep's conclusion: without knowing the size of losses and the price of future capital raises, intrinsic value per share could not be determined. The apparent cheapness was therefore the investor's Achilles heel. Nomad sold its MBIA position.

### The Institutional Failure
Short sellers understood this dynamic:

> "The shorts know the institutions are weak minded. And the institutions themselves know they are weak... They are weak because, although equity is permanent capital, the owners of the equity are short-term oriented." — Letter 14, June 2008

Institutions reasoned: why take a stand when the shares could be half the price if I wait? And why buy now when I might get shares in a placing at an even lower price? This abdication of ownership, combined with the US preference for placings over rights issues (which cut existing shareholders out of capital-raising altogether), ensures that the early contrarian investor — who is too small to recap the business on their own — is severely punished.

### The Wilbur Ross Solution
Sleep contrasts the institutional abdication with what Assured Guaranty arranged with Wilbur Ross: Ross agreed to provide up to US&#36;750m as, when, and if the company required it — in effect underwriting the equity value. As a result, Assured Guaranty's shares traded around book value while all other bond insurers, including MBIA, traded well below.

The lesson: dilution risk "virtually disappears if large institutional shareholders backstop capital raising at reasonable prices." This requires a "firmly held view of what a company is worth" and is best done by way of rights (with rights not taken up underwritten by large holders). This is part of the [[Active_Ownership]] argument: size matters not just for governance influence but for capital stability.

### Implications for Nomad's Portfolio Construction
After the MBIA experience, Sleep notes that Nomad's recent purchases — which were not large enough to recap businesses on their own — have been "modest in size" where there is meaningful dilution risk. This is a self-limiting discipline: if you cannot protect yourself from dilution (by backstopping capital raises), do not own large positions in companies that may need to raise capital at distressed prices.

Contrast this with Nomad's major positions in [[Scale_Economies_Shared]] companies like [[Amazon]] and [[Costco_Wholesale]], which generate surplus free cash flow and do not face dilution risk at all — the combination of a strong balance sheet and a business model that self-funds growth eliminates this vulnerability.
