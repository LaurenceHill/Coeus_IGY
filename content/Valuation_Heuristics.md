# Valuation Heuristics

## Summary
In Letter 7 (December 2004), [[Nick_Sleep]] launches a sustained critique of the investment industry's reliance on "heuristics" — simple rules of thumb like price-to-book (P/B), price-to-earnings (P/E), and EV/EBITDA — as substitutes for real business analysis. His argument: these metrics are intellectually lazy, frequently misleading, and have nothing to do with the correct definition of value. They survive not because they work but because they are easy to sell to clients and require minimal thinking.

## Explanation

### The Correct Definition of Value
Sleep quotes [[Warren_Buffett]]'s 1992 Berkshire Hathaway Annual Report:

> "Whether appropriate or not, the term 'value investing' is widely used. Typically, it connotes the purchase of stocks having attributes such as a low ratio of price to book value, a low price-earnings ratio, or a high dividend yield. Unfortunately, such characteristics, even if they appear in combination, are far from determinative as to whether an investor is indeed buying something for what it is worth... Correspondingly, opposite characteristics — a high ratio of price to book value, a high price-earnings ratio, a low dividend yield — are in no way inconsistent with a 'value' purchase."

Sleep's own formulation: **"a business is worth the free cash flow that it can be expected to generate between now and judgment day, discounted back at a reasonable rate."** This is the only definition that is correct. Everything else is a heuristic.

### Why Heuristics Persist
Sleep draws on psychology. Heuristics work in everyday life — we don't consciously process every option when getting dressed. But in investing, "doing half the work... is of limited benefit if successful investment requires more work." Their persistence is attributed to:

1. **Intellectual laziness** on the part of investment professionals.
2. **Marketing spin** — simple metrics are easier to explain to clients and make the process look systematic.
3. **Survivorship** — as a minority sport, successful investing based on real analysis is rare; heuristic-based analysis fills the vacuum.

### The Growth/Value False Dichotomy
Sleep argues the growth vs. value debate is "perennial and quite unnecessary." It is based entirely on heuristic thinking: growth investors use high P/E and high P/B; value investors use low P/E and low P/B. Both are measuring the wrong thing.

> "When commentators suggest that 'value' has beaten 'growth', or 'growth' has beaten 'value', please note that little of real substance is being imparted."

The correct insight — which Buffett had "years ago" — is that **growth is inherently part of the value judgment**, not a separate discipline. A business growing at 20% p.a. might be far cheaper than a static business, even if the former has a higher P/E.

### Three Heuristics Incorrectly Applied to Costco
Sleep uses [[Costco_Wholesale]] as the canonical demonstration of how heuristics mislead:

1. **"The company has low margins"** (net profit margin of 1.7% vs Wal-Mart 3.6% and Target 4.2%). True — but the low margin is the *point*. The firm defers profits to extend franchise life and competitiveness. See [[Scale_Economies_Shared]].
2. **"It's expensive at 24x earnings"**. But if Costco raised prices to Wal-Mart margins, it would trade at 11x. The 24x is a reflection of deliberate under-earning, not overvaluation.
3. **"Costco has a cost problem"**. Rising costs reflect investment in warehouse and distribution infrastructure for the next phase of growth — transient, not structural.

The result: "one in five analysts rate the stock a buy" — creating the mispricing Sleep exploits.

### Historical Failure Modes of Heuristic Thinking
Sleep illustrates with two cases:
- **K-Mart**: perpetually "cheap" on P/B, terrible investment for decades.
- **Dell in 1990**: not expensive by growth standards, but value investors wouldn't touch it because they couldn't value growth. Those who sold IBM in the 1950s made the same error.

The question Sleep frames: what is it about your approach that would have stopped you owning K-Mart for twenty years? And what would have stopped you selling Wal-Mart? The answer requires analysing "not the effects and outputs of a business, but the underlying reality — the engine of its success."

### The Ayn Rand Principle
Sleep quotes John Galt's speech from *Atlas Shrugged*: "no substitute can do your thinking." Applied to investing: no ratio or heuristic can substitute for the work of understanding a business's underlying economic reality.

> "Watch out, this industry is full of the non-thought of received ideas, starting with the value/growth debate and valuation heuristics." — Letter 7, December 2004

### The Keynes Warning
Sleep also cites Keynes's *General Theory*:
> "Worldly wisdom teaches that it is better for reputation to fail conventionally than to succeed unconventionally."

This is the institutional pressure that perpetuates heuristics: it is career-safe to be wrong using the same tools as everyone else.
