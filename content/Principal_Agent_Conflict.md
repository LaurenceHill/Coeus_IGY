# Principal-Agent Conflict

## Summary
The principal-agent conflict — the divergence of interests between the owner of capital (the principal) and the manager of that capital (the agent) — is one of [[Nick_Sleep]]'s most frequently recurring themes across the Nomad letters. He identifies it as the root cause of most structural dysfunctions in the investment industry: over-diversification, short-termism, quarterly reporting, asset gathering at the expense of performance, and exploitative fee structures. His response at [[Nomad_Investment_Partnership]] is to systematically minimise the conflict by behaving like a principal wherever possible.

## Explanation

### The Two Approaches
> "There are two ways to approach this situation: 1. maximise the conflict for the sake of maximising short-term agent revenues (standard industry practice), or 2. set about minimising the difference through behaving and thinking like principals. Zak and I have followed the second path." — Letter 9, December 2005

Standard industry practice maximises the conflict: managers earn asset-based fees regardless of performance, use diversification to protect their careers, and chase AUM growth to maximise revenue. All of these serve the agent's interests at the expense of the principal's long-run compounding.

### The Performance Fee Problem — Letter 9
Sleep identified a specific structural problem with Nomad's own performance fee: by December 2005, the fund was so far ahead of the 6% per annum compounding hurdle that even if future returns fell to passbook rates (say 5% p.a.) for thirty years, a "performance fee" would still accrue. During those thirty years Sleep would add no value over money-market rates — yet would still be paid for "performance."

> "We are only in this position because we have done so well, and one could argue that contractually we have earned the right by dint of performance, but just look at the conflicts!" — Letter 9, December 2005

His proposed fix: amend the high-water mark so that it also **compounds at 6% per annum**. That way each new year has a genuine 6% hurdle, and passbook-rate returns go unrewarded. No one pressured this change — Sleep proposed it voluntarily because he did not want to earn a fee for performance below 6% p.a.

### The Refundable Performance Fee — Letter 10
After leaving [[Marathon_Asset_Management]] and forming [[Sleep_Zakaria_and_Company]], Sleep and Zakaria proposed a **refundable performance fee**: fees would be deferred and subject to repayment in the event of subsequent underperformance. The mechanism: bank the performance fees, drip them to the managers if performance remains adequate, or drip reimbursements back to investors if performance deteriorates.

This structure was so non-standard that a large global bank told Sleep they did not have the capacity to service it, despite earning a 25% return on equity. The FSA, by contrast, approved Sleep, Zakaria and Company's operating licence in four weeks (vs. the typical 3–4 months), partly due to Nomad's operational simplicity.

### Fund Sizing and the Right to Shrink
Sleep articulates an obligation that almost no fund manager discusses: the duty to **return capital** if the opportunity set shrinks:

> "If we find that the opportunity set is small... our ability to expand and shrink will be an important tool sometime in the next twenty years. I guarantee it." — Letters 9 & 10

He frames it with a bus metaphor: "the bus is always running at full speed with the number of passengers optimised to maintain maximum speed." Piling on paying passengers until the bus slows to a dangerous crawl in the slow lane — the Calcutta commuter bus — is the industry's sin.

The reopening rule (Letter 10): Nomad will only reopen if incremental new dollars bring something to the party — specifically, by **lowering the price-to-value ratio of the Partnership as a whole**. Otherwise, growth serves the agent (management fee in dollars rises) but harms the principal (dilutes returns).

### Investor Ranking and the "Right Crowd"
Capital raises (when they occur) follow a queue where:
- Principals are ranked above agents
- No preference is given for the size of the investor's pot

The stated aim: maintain the Partnership's investor base as people who "see Nomad in the same light." The ethos is borrowed from the Brooklands Motor Racing Circuit:
> "The right crowd, and no crowding."

### Fee Structure at Sleep, Zakaria and Company
The management fee is structured as a **cost reimbursement**, not a profit centre:
- Estimated 0.7–1% of NAV p.a., capped at 1%
- Sleep and Zakaria took pay cuts to set this up; any deficit is funded by the managers personally
- As the Partnership grows, the management fee declines as a percentage of assets → all investors share in the natural scale economics of the operation

Sleep and Zakaria also structured [[Sleep_Zakaria_and_Company]] so they **cannot sell the fund to another manager**: "You can sack us, but we won't sell you."

### The Management Fee as Industry Critique — Letter 22
By Letter 22 (June 2012), [[Nomad_Investment_Partnership]]'s cost-reimbursement management fee had fallen from approximately **50 basis points to near 10 basis points** over six years — without any reduction in quality of operation. Sleep and Zakaria normalised salaries, travelled as needed, purchased valued research, and paid market rent. The decline was entirely a product of scale: as NAV grew, fixed costs divided across a larger base, lowering the percentage.

Sleep quantifies the benefit to partners — predominantly charities and endowments for charitable causes:
- **~US&#36;15m per annum** saved vs. the industry-standard 1% management fee
- **~US&#36;30m per annum** saved vs. the hedge fund standard

> "We mention this to pop the illusion that investment management is a Giffen good (one where, paradoxically, demand rises with price)." — Letter 22, June 2012

The deeper critique: "Incentives (and rules) can de-moralise behaviour, and what the industry needs more than anything, perhaps, is a sense of right and wrong. It can be hard to separate right from wrong when there is so much money sloshing around." Sleep speculates that lowering management fees across the peer group might improve aggregate industry behaviour in the process — not merely as a nice idea, but as a structural fix to misaligned incentives.

### The 6% Hurdle That Is Never 6% — Letter 24
Letter 24 (June 2013) revisits the performance fee structure with a candid mathematical admission. The hurdle rate of 6% per annum, chosen to reflect the opportunity cost of long bonds at the time the fee was designed, is in practice never actually 6%. Two reasons:

1. **Mathematical asymmetry**: A linked 10% loss and a 10% gain are not equivalent — the gain must be larger than the loss to restore the original sum. The compounding hurdle therefore drifts above 6% in most sequences.
2. **Fee reserve investment**: The performance fee reserve is itself invested in Nomad shares, meaning it accrues (buys) during good performance and is reimbursed (sells) during poor performance — structurally forced to buy high and sell low.

Sleep notes that Zakaria "has set himself the task of finding a case when the six percent hurdle might be less than six percent. He may be some time."

Despite this, Sleep is "quite relaxed" about the imprecision: the hurdle was chosen for philosophical reasons (opportunity cost of capital; extended performance fee risk), not for mathematical exactness. The response is characteristic of [[Non_Monetary_Incentives]]: Sleep and Zakaria derive enough meaning from the work — "challenge, sense of job well done, identity, creativity" — that the precise calibration of financial reward matters less than the underlying philosophy.

> "It is not very important to us that a six percent hurdle has been well over ten percent in the recent past, and we would certainly never do anything to lower it." — Letter 24, June 2013

### Why the Principal-Agent Problem is Worsening
Sleep argues (Letter 9) that the business-oriented evolution of the investment industry — more hedge funds, more agents, more quarterly reporting — is likely **widening** the principal-agent conflict over time. This is paradoxically good news for Nomad: the wider the conflict becomes across the industry, the more the psychological misjudgements it produces (social proof, short-termism, liquidity fetishisation) will create mispricings for patient, aligned investors to exploit.
