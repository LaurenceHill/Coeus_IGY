# Kelly Criterion

## Summary
The Kelly Criterion is a mathematical formula for optimal bet-sizing, cited by [[Nick_Sleep]] in Letter 6 (June 2004) to argue that [[Nomad_Investment_Partnership]]'s portfolio has at times been **too diversified**, not too concentrated. Originally devised by J.L. Kelly in 1956 and popularised by Bill Miller (Legg Mason Value Trust), it provides a theoretical benchmark for how much of a portfolio should be allocated to a given idea based on conviction.

## Explanation

### The Formula
Sleep gives a simplified version:

> **Optimal bet = 2.1 × p – 1.1**

where **p** is the probability of being right (i.e., the investment working out).

| Probability of being right (p) | Optimal portfolio allocation |
|---|---|
| 100% (certain) | 100% |
| 75% (high conviction) | 47.5% |
| 55% (slight edge) | 5.5% |
| 52.4% (break-even) | 0% |
| <52.4% | Don't bet |

The result is striking: at 75% conviction — which a serious fundamental investor might feel about their best ideas — the Kelly-optimal position is **47.5% of the portfolio**. This is far beyond what any conventional fund manager would hold in a single position.

### Sleep's Application: The Industry Is Over-Diversified

Sleep uses the Kelly Criterion not as a precise allocation tool but as a **diagnostic** for the industry's approach to [[Portfolio_Concentration|portfolio construction]]:

> "Apply the Kelly criterion, and the average fund manager would appear to have almost no clue as to the likely success of any one idea." — Letter 6, June 2004

A fund holding 200 stocks equally weighted implies roughly 0.5% per position — which, working back through Kelly, implies the manager believes each idea has perhaps a 53% chance of working. That is barely an edge at all. Sleep's conclusion: institutional over-diversification is not driven by analytical humility about conviction levels. It is driven by **marketing** (making clients comfortable), **career protection** (no single idea can destroy the fund or the manager's career), and **smoothing of results** — none of which is in the client's long-run interest.

### The Historical Precedent: Early Buffett Partnership
The only historical investment vehicles Sleep cites as approaching Kelly-optimal concentration are the **early Buffett Partnership** portfolios — where Buffett did hold very high concentrations, primarily in companies where he was a controlling shareholder. Sleep sees this as the logically correct approach, even if almost no one practises it.

### What This Means for Nomad
Sleep draws the explicit conclusion:

> "The logical extension of this line of thought is that Nomad's portfolio concentration has at times been too low." — Letter 6, June 2004

This is a remarkably honest self-critique. Despite Nomad holding a much more concentrated portfolio than the industry average, Sleep believes even *it* has been under-concentrated relative to the Kelly optimum. The practical constraint is not conviction but **liquidity and position availability** — as the [[Union_Cement]] episode illustrates (Sleep wanted a 10% position but could only buy 1% due to thin trading).

### The Right Goal: Volatility vs. Long-Run Returns
Sleep explicitly states his preference:

> "At Nomad we would rather results were more volatile year to year but maximised our rolling five-year outcome." — Letter 6, June 2004

This is a direct rejection of the industry's smoothing logic. Year-to-year volatility is irrelevant to long-run compounding; in fact, reducing volatility through diversification reduces expected return.

### Letter 7 Application: Costco as a Live Example
In Letter 7 (December 2004), Sleep returns to Kelly in the context of [[Costco_Wholesale]], which by that point is ~10% of Nomad's assets. He explicitly states this position is likely *too small*:

> "If one applies the Kelly criterion (discussed in the last letter) the weighting should be much higher. So already your manager may have made his first mistake investing in Costco. From not buying enough." — Letter 7, December 2004

At ~75% conviction on Costco's thesis (the predictability of the [[Scale_Economies_Shared]] model, the low probability of failure given self-funded growth), Kelly implies an optimal allocation of ~47.5%. A 10% actual position is less than a quarter of that.

Sleep frames this honestly: the gap between the Kelly-optimal and the actual position is partly his error (he could have added more at US&#36;30), and partly a structural constraint (once orders are aggregated with Marathon's main funds, Nomad effectively behaves as a US&#36;30bn fund and moves markets).

## Contradictions / Notes
- Sleep acknowledges the Kelly Criterion is "simplified" and presents it as a directional argument, not a precise portfolio management tool.
- The 2.1× multiplier in the formula is a specific constant from Kelly's derivation under certain assumptions — Sleep does not elaborate on these assumptions in the letters.
