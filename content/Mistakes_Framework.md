# Mistakes Framework

## Summary
[[Nick_Sleep]]'s framework for thinking about investment mistakes, introduced in Letter 13 (December 2007), argues that the correct response to a mistake is non-judgmental forgiveness combined with rigorous learning — not self-condemnation or the structural incentives for blame that the financial industry typically provides. Mistakes become "net present value positive" if the lessons from them are properly extracted and applied. Three types of mistake — denial, anchoring, and drift — are identified as the most common sources of serious analytical errors.

## Explanation

### The Right Attitude: Non-Judgmental Forgiveness
> "In dealing with mistakes the best state of mind is non-judgmental forgiveness." — Letter 13, December 2007

Sleep uses the analogy of parenting: if a child "thinks right, they will make mistakes, work it out for themselves and learn. They do not need to be judged or punished: instead, they need support." Once lessons are learnt, the mistakes are small compared to what is gained. In investment terms:

> "Once lessons have been learnt, mistakes can be put on a price earnings ratio of one and the resultant, conditioned, good behaviour on a ratio of more than one. In other words, mistakes become net present value positive." — Letter 13, December 2007

By contrast, the financial industry is structured to allow "embarrassment, frustration and anger to vent through the various asset classes" — selling a damaged fund to buy a new one, switching from tech to gold, and so on. This is judgmental rather than forgiving behaviour, and it prevents learning.

### Three Cognitive Traps

**1. Denial**
Reinventing reality in the mind because the truth is too painful to bear. Companies regularly misclassify their mistakes in terms of outputs rather than inputs — "we cannot do commercial property" rather than "we had poor credit underwriting standards." Sleep's Bank X case: after losing money on commercial property loans in the early 1990s, the bank concluded it "could not do commercial property" and diversified into investment banking and international markets. But the real mistake was not the asset class — it was the *cognitive approach* to underwriting risk. By condemning the output and not rewiring the underlying process, the mistake was left unchecked. The result: shares rose ~10% over the next 10 years (~1% per year), well below a deposit account.

**2. Anchoring**
A static, historic vision of a problem — specifically, forming a view at the time of purchase and then failing to evolve it as facts change. This was the common root of both [[Nomad_Investment_Partnership]]'s biggest errors: [[Conseco]] (anchor on original analysis; held through bankruptcy) and [[Stagecoach_Group]] (anchor on original target price; sold at ~90p, by Letter 13 above £2.50). See [[Destination_Analysis]].

The Keynes quote — "better to be generally right than precisely wrong" — is the antidote. Sleep notes: "At the time we were making these errors we would have held Keynes' quote as true. One has to be so careful; sometimes these mistakes are very insidious."

**3. Drift**
Small incremental changes in thinking that build into a large mistake. Sleep cites the Buffett golf story as the antidote:

> Warren Buffett was offered a bet: shoot a hole-in-one on an 18-hole course and win US&#36;10,000; fail and pay US&#36;10. Buffett declined. Why? "If you are not disciplined in the little things, you won't be disciplined in the big things." — Walter Schloss, *Outstanding Investor Digest*, June 23, 1989

Buffett was watching out for drift — the gradual erosion of discipline through small lapses. Sleep connects this to corporate language corruption (banks calling losses "market turbulence," companies calling revenue declines "negative growth") — once language drifts, perception of reality drifts too. The biggest mistakes "did not look like mistakes at the beginning."

### The MDC vs Bank X Case Study
Two firms were caught by the same 1990s property downturn:

**MDC (Larry Mizel):** Learnt correctly. Identified the real error (buying land at cycle peaks with too much debt). Since the 1990s: minimal land bank, conservative debt, equity capital returned to shareholders via dividends and buybacks. Share price rose **10-fold in 10 years** despite industry-wide declines.

**Bank X:** Learnt incorrectly (denial/output misclassification). Concluded it "could not do commercial property" but diversified internationally and into investment banking instead of fixing the credit process. Share price up ~**10% in 10 years** (~1%/year) — underperforming a deposit account at its own institution.

The lesson: the response to the mistake is often more important than the mistake itself.

### Diversification as Denial
Sleep embeds a second lesson in the MDC/Bank X comparison: the popular wisdom that diversification lowers risk is a form of denial. Bank X diversified *instead of* fixing the underlying problem. More broadly:

> "Its ascendancy into the unchallengeable heights of received wisdom comes from the vivid image of seeing one's nest egg disappear because of one mistake. What is not recorded is the cost of the suboptimal outcomes that result from over-diversification." — Letter 13, December 2007

Securitisation trusts (the vehicle for the 2008 financial crisis) are the ultimate expression of this: "diversification used in this way tries to turn ignorance into an asset." See [[Diworsification]].

### Investing in Businesses that Have Made Mistakes
Sleep applies the framework by deliberately investing in companies that have made mistakes and learnt from them:

> "Our current investments in Liberty Global, Games Workshop, Dell, Sony and Ford fit into this category, as do previous investments in firms in bankruptcy such as Telewest (now Virgin Media) and Calpine." — Letter 13, December 2007

What is required: "a rational, honest mea culpa and a low share price. The unforgiving nature of the markets often provides the latter. The former is much harder to come by." The Games Workshop chairman's frank public admission in the 2007 accounts — "We grew fat and lazy on the back of easy success" — is cited in Letter 14 as exactly the right response.

### Say-Something Syndrome
Sleep uses the Rockefeller/Schloss episode to argue that disclosing too much can be a mistake in itself:

- Rockefeller: "Weak men had loose tongues and blabbed to reporters, whilst prudent businessmen kept their own counsel"
- Walter Schloss and the Buffett Partnership did not disclose holdings; Walter Schloss's anecdote about the partner who withdrew (then died) after seeing the portfolio
- Six-monthly letter writing "may be as irrational as monthly reporting" — a Charles Lockhart disposition would favour ad hoc writing when one has something to say

The psychological problem with disclosure: locking in ideas, desire for consistency, copy-cat investing, brokers trading against the portfolio. But Sleep resolves: holdings will continue to be disclosed (under separate post) to the subset of partners who study them with "a joy for the art of investing."
