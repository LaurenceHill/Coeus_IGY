# Active Ownership (Shareholder Engagement)

## Summary
Active ownership is [[Nick_Sleep]]'s term for the practice of large, properly oriented shareholders using their influence to improve corporate behaviour, resist bad management decisions, and recover value on behalf of all shareholders. It is the natural complement to [[Portfolio_Concentration]]: only when a fund holds a meaningful stake (Sleep suggests >10%) can it actually influence outcomes. Letter 6 (June 2004) provides four live examples; Letter 11 (December 2006) adds three UK cases including Nomad becoming the largest shareholder in Games Workshop and a 19% dissenting bloc at [[Whitehead_Mann]].

## Explanation

### The Principle
Sleep frames large shareholders as "general partners" in the businesses they own — not passive price-takers but active co-owners with both the right and the responsibility to engage:

> "The influence that a large properly oriented outside shareholder can have over corporate behaviour should not be under-estimated." — Letter 6, June 2004

This sits in direct contrast to the standard fund management response to a bad management decision: sell the shares and buy something else. Sleep's view: selling does no one any good and leaves bad management in place to harm the next investor.

### Four Live Examples (Letter 6)

**1. Jardine Matheson / Jardine Strategic**
Brandes Investment Partners (largest outside shareholder) proposed a corporate reorganisation; [[Marathon_Asset_Management]] seconded the motion. Management voted against it via their shares in sister company Jardine Strategic — the motion failed. But a "watered-down version" of the Brandes strategy was subsequently pursued, with "wonderful results" for shareholders.

Sleep notes Marathon's role as seconder — not leader — of the campaign. The distinction matters: active ownership does not require a fund to run activist campaigns solo; it requires alignment with other engaged shareholders.

**2. Kersaf (South African casino)**
Allan Gray Limited (Allan Gray's fund) and Marathon combined votes to propose an about-turn in company strategy. The strategy — described as "all Allan Gray's work, not ours" — proved excellent. The Kersaf share price doubled in rand and rose ~4× in US dollars for Nomad. Sleep credits the outcome entirely to Allan Gray's work, and Marathon's vote was enabling rather than driving.

**3. Hollinger International**
Tweedy, Browne Company professionally pursued former Chairman Conrad Black through a governance campaign to recover funds paid to him. In the process, the private market value of Hollinger's newspaper properties was realised, resulting in a **doubling of the shares from Nomad's purchase price**. Sleep notes Nomad's own "input has been limited to private correspondence with Lord Black, which is framed and may be viewed at Marathon's offices" — a dry aside that acknowledges they were not the lead actor.

**4. HCI / eTV (South Africa)**
Marathon was actively resisting in Court an attempt by management to buy out the firm (HCI, owner of South Africa's independent commercial TV station eTV) at what Sleep calls "demonstrably — and management agree — a massive discount to its real value." Sleep frames this as the appropriate response:

> "To stand by and watch whilst we are having our wallet lifted makes little sense to us. Selling the shares and buying something else does no one any good either, even though this is common practice in the industry." — Letter 6, June 2004

### The Weetabix Counter-Example
The [[Weetabix_Limited]] Hicks Muse takeout (Letter 5) is the cautionary tale Sleep uses to motivate the need for active ownership:

> "The best defence is to own enough of the company to influence the outcome. In most cases in excess of 10% of the shares outstanding would suffice." — Letter 5, December 2003

Nomad's stake in Weetabix was too small to block the Scheme of Arrangement. The lesson: position size matters not just for return but for influence. Sleep explicitly states his intention: as Nomad grows, he will not repeat this mistake.

### The Industry Critique
Sleep contrasts active ownership with the industry norm of passive acceptance:

> "Selling the shares and buying something else does no one any good either, even though this is common practice in the industry." — Letter 6, June 2004

The conventional fund management playbook is: if management misbehave, express displeasure by selling (the "Wall Street walk"). This removes the engaged shareholder from the register but does nothing to change management behaviour, and leaves the next investor to face the same problem.

### The 10% Threshold
Sleep repeatedly cites 10%+ ownership as the level required to meaningfully influence outcomes. Below this, management can disregard external shareholders. Above it, a shareholder has voting power that matters in Schemes of Arrangement, extraordinary general meetings, and board elections. This threshold explains the [[Portfolio_Concentration]] argument: owning 30+ positions at 0.5%–2% each makes genuine active ownership impossible.

### UK Active Ownership — Letter 11 (December 2006)
Sleep had explicitly committed in Letter 5: "Should Nomad continue to grow in size, we intend not to make this mistake [of owning too little to influence outcomes]." By Letter 11, that commitment was being enacted:

**Games Workshop Plc.** — Nomad is now the **largest shareholder** in the UK wargames miniature company. No detail given on the specific engagement thesis in Letter 11; the position is flagged as part of "interesting UK investments where we may have some influence on corporate development."

**Jarvis Plc.** — Nomad is a **significant shareholder**. Again, engagement is described as nascent rather than concluded.

**[[Whitehead_Mann]] Plc.** — The most detailed case study of Letter 11. An executive search firm (largest headhunter to FTSE 100 CEOs) whose management declined to invest in the bankruptcy-rescuing rights placing at 40p, then subsequently offered to buy the entire company at 42p — a 5% premium to the rescue price, capturing the upside for themselves at the expense of the shareholders who saved the company.

Nomad joined a dissenting bloc of approximately **19% of shares outstanding** — enough under UK law to block compulsory acquisition. Sleep's valuation approach for the now-delisted holding: carry at 42p plus (or minus) retained earnings. "We look forward to seeing what management can do for us, now they have (leveraged) skin in the game."

The Whitehead Mann episode reinforces that active ownership sometimes means **staying in** an illiquid or unlisted position rather than selling. The willingness to hold unlisted equity (enabled by the Nomad mandate expansion in January 2003, Letter 4) is what makes this kind of dissent possible.

### The Pattern: Private Equity and Insider Bids (Letter 11)
Sleep describes 2006 as a record year for private equity. He frames many management buyouts as "insider trading with the takeover rules being used as a bulk purchasing facility" — managements who understand the business's true value use their information advantage to buy out minority shareholders at a discount. Active ownership — owning enough to say no — is the only defence.

> "In no other sphere of capitalism can your property be seized in exchange for cents on the dollar... Fund managers, who in their private capacity would be insulted if someone offered less than their house was worth, happily sell shares in their professional capacity at discounted prices to smart buyers. And no one cries foul." — Letter 11, December 2006
