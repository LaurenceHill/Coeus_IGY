# Scale Economies Shared

## Summary
Scale Economies Shared is [[Nick_Sleep]]'s term for the most powerful business model he has identified: companies that generate scale efficiencies but deliberately pass them back to customers rather than retaining them as profits. The model's paradox is that the company grows *through* giving more back. First named explicitly in Letter 8 (June 2005), it is Sleep's primary framework for understanding [[Costco_Wholesale]] and is one of only a "handful" of distinct mental models he uses to evaluate investments. By Letter 8, this model (together with "deep discount to replacement cost") accounts for ~45% of Nomad's total assets.

## Explanation

### The Core Observation
> "Most companies pursue scale efficiencies, but few share them. It's the sharing that makes the model so powerful." — Letter 7, December 2004

As a company grows, unit costs fall. The standard corporate response is to pocket this improvement as margin expansion. The Scale Economies Shared model inverts this: cost savings are returned to the customer as lower prices, which attracts more customers, which drives more volume, which drives more scale, which enables more savings to be returned. The cycle is self-reinforcing and — crucially — **self-defending**.

### The Competitive Moat
The model creates an extraordinarily durable competitive advantage because:

1. **Matching requires enormous sacrifice.** A competitor wishing to match Costco's prices and wages would need to absorb losses of approximately US&#36;2.2bn annually (US&#36;1.4bn to match prices + US&#36;750m to match pay scales) — not insignificant even for Wal-Mart.
2. **The gap widens over time.** As the incumbent grows further, its scale advantage increases, enabling yet more sharing with customers, yet further widening the gap.
3. **Customer stickiness is intrinsic.** The customer has an economic reason to return that is independent of advertising or brand perception. They are participating in the model.

Sleep explicitly places this in the whiteboard of models he has found to "work and that we can understand":
> "In the office we have a white board on which we have listed the (very few) investment models that work and that we can understand. Costco is the best example we can find of one of them: scale efficiencies shared." — Letter 7, December 2004

### The Paradox
> "But in the center of the model is a paradox: the company grows through giving more back." — Letter 7, December 2004

Sleep asks: what would a company say if asked what it would do with windfall profits? Almost universally, companies would spend, invest, or return cash to shareholders. Almost none would reply "give it back to customers." And yet that is precisely what makes the model so rare and powerful.

### Misreading by the Market
The model is chronically misread by Wall Street because it appears, using conventional metrics, to be a low-margin, "expensive" business with a "cost problem." Three heuristics are incorrectly applied (see [[Valuation_Heuristics]]):
1. **"Low margins"** — The low margin *is the product*. It defers profit today to extend franchise life.
2. **"Expensive at 24x earnings"** — If Costco raised prices to Wal-Mart levels, it would trade at 11x. The 24x is a reflection of deliberate under-earning.
3. **"Cost problem"** — Rising costs are investment in growth infrastructure (warehouses, distribution), not structural inefficiency.

### Scale Economies Shared vs. Other Models
In Letter 8, Sleep explicitly states he has only a "handful of distinct investment models" and names two that together make up ~45% of Nomad's assets:
1. **Scale Economies Shared** — Costco, Amazon.com
2. **[[Deep_Discount_to_Replacement_Cost]]** — Zimbabwe cement; Asian crisis investments

The Amazon.com comparison is important: Amazon follows a similar model of reinvesting all efficiency gains back into customer experience and prices rather than retaining profits. Sleep sees this as early evidence that eBay may be an even purer version (marketplace structure, natural monopoly, near-zero incremental asset cost).

### The "Compounding Machine" Quality
Sleep describes Costco as "retail's version of perpetual motion" — a self-sustaining engine where the inputs (scale) generate outputs (customer savings and loyalty) that in turn drive greater inputs (more scale). This is the direct ancestor of what he later calls the [[Terminal_Portfolio]] concept — businesses that can be held indefinitely because the internal logic of their model continuously compounds without intervention.

## Measurement: The [[Robustness_Ratio]]
In Letter 8, Sleep introduces a quantitative proxy for moat width in Scale Economies Shared businesses: the **robustness ratio** — the ratio of savings to customers (and benefits to employees) relative to earnings retained by shareholders. For Costco the ratio is ~5:1; for GEICO it is ~1:1. Both represent powerful moats but for different reasons. See [[Robustness_Ratio]] for the full framework.

### The Dominant Portfolio Model — Letters 11–12
By December 2006 (Letter 11), Sleep describes Scale Economies Shared businesses as having come to **dominate Nomad**:

> "The scale efficiencies shared model... is deployed by companies which have now come to dominate Nomad: Costco, Dell, Amazon and Berkshire (GEICO, Nebraska Furniture Mart)." — Letter 11, December 2006

The [[Amazon]] case is the most fully developed argument for the model in the letters because Amazon's founder, Jeff Bezos, articulates it explicitly in his own annual report:

> "Our judgment is that relentlessly returning efficiency improvements and scale economies to customers in the form of lower prices creates a virtuous cycle that leads over the long-term to a much larger dollar amount of free cash flow, and thereby to a much more valuable Amazon.com." — Jeff Bezos, 2005 Amazon Annual Report (quoted Letter 11)

Bezos acknowledges that this decision "cannot be made in a math-based way" — the short-term math always says raise prices. The long-term judgment says pass the savings on. Sleep's observation: "I think Bezos would run a good investment fund: but that is the point, good investing and good business decisions are synonymous."

The controversy: "As our shareholders know..." Bezos writes. Sleep notes that "judging by the share volumes — they don't!" This is precisely the mispricing opportunity: the market anchors on near-term free cash flow while missing the moat being built.

### Link to Scaling Laws
In Letter 12, Sleep adds a structural dimension to the model via [[Scaling_Laws]]: companies with a *simple skeletal structure* (like Amazon's internet model vs. traditional high street retail) can scale from mouse to elephant without breakdown. When Scale Economies Shared is combined with a high-power-law skeletal structure, the competitive moat widens on two axes simultaneously — price advantage and structural scalability.

### Air Asia — SES Outside Retail — Letter 14 (June 2008)
[[Air_Asia]] is Sleep's most developed non-retail application of the Scale Economies Shared model. By Letter 14, Air Asia joined Amazon, Costco, Dell, Berkshire (GEICO, Nebraska Furniture Mart) as the core of the SES portfolio cluster — representing approximately **45% of total Nomad assets** across these companies.

Air Asia's structural cost advantage: ~3 US cents per seat per kilometre (including fuel, December 2007) vs. ~4.5 cents for Ryanair and ~5.5 cents for Southwest — the lowest cost per seat per km of any airline globally. The advantage is shared with customers via below-competitor fares, which keeps planes full, which reinforces the cost advantage. The mechanism is identical to Costco's.

Sleep notes that "Average revenue per seat kilometre at Air Asia is below the cost per seat kilometre at Malaysian Airlines" — meaning Air Asia's ticket prices are cheaper than its flag-carrier competitor's *cost*. A competitor cannot match Air Asia on price without losing money.

### The Sol Price 1967 Memo — Letter 18 (June 2010)
Letter 18 adds the deepest historical root of the SES model. When visiting [[Costco_Wholesale]] CEO Jim Sinegal, Sleep was shown a 1967 memo written by Sol Price, founder of Fed-Mart (the company that directly preceded Costco):

> "Although we are all interested in margin, it must never be done at the expense of our philosophy. Margin must be obtained by better buying, emphasis on selling the kind of goods we want to sell, operating efficiencies, lower markdowns, greater turnover, etc. Increasing the retail prices and justifying it on the basis that we are still 'competitive' could lead to a rude awakening as it has with so many. **Let us concentrate on how cheap we can bring things to the people, rather than how much the traffic will bear**, and when the race is over Fed-Mart will be there." — Sol Price, 1967

Sleep calls this "the best summary of the business case for scale economics shared we have come across." A copy is framed on Sleep and Zakaria's office wall. The memo was written 43 years before Letter 18 — and 43 years later, Costco is the world's most valuable retailer of its type. The culture encoded in those 103 words is the SES model's philosophical foundation. See [[Interlocking_Advantages]] and [[Character_as_Moat]].

### Crisis Validation — Letter 15 (December 2008)
The 2008–2009 recession provided a live test of the SES model's durability:
- **Amazon**: Christmas 2008 orders up +16% while total US retail sales fell ~10%. Trailing twelve-month revenues +60% since mid-2007. The business was "not participating" in the recession.
- **Air Asia**: Revenue per seat per kilometre rose +33%; costs per seat per kilometre (excluding fuel) fell -10%. The opposite of conventional airline economics in a downturn.

Sleep's inference: SES businesses outperform in recessions because the customer benefit of low prices becomes *more* valuable when discretionary budgets tighten. Price-leadership is a recession hedge.

### Advertising as the Price of Mediocrity — Letter 16 (June 2009)
Sleep quotes Jeff Bezos directly: "Advertising is the price you pay for having an unremarkable product or service." This is the clearest statement of the SES model's implicit logic: a company that has genuinely shared its scale advantage with customers does not need to advertise — the customer returns because the deal is self-evidently good.

Sleep notes that approximately **two-thirds of Nomad's portfolio** at Letter 16 is in companies that shun promotional activity or spend minimally on advertising. This is a deliberate screen: excessive advertising spend is a signal that the customer proposition requires artificial support.

The contrast with conventional high-low retailing captures the incentive problem: high-low retailers train customers to buy on deal, creating volatile volumes and rising costs. SES retailers create customer loyalty through structural price leadership.

### A Century-Old Model — Letter 22 (June 2012)
Letter 22 provides the deepest historical grounding for the SES model via a 1927 Franklin Institute report on Henry Ford. The report includes a chart tracking Ford's Model T from 1908 to 1924:
- **Line A**: production volumes rising from zero to over 2 million cars per annum
- **Line B**: cost to the consumer falling from US&#36;950 to ~US&#36;300

Sleep's observation: the business model that built the Ford empire a century ago is structurally identical to the one that built Wal-Mart, Southwest Airlines, and Amazon. Bezos makes explicit why the model is permanent:

> "I can't imagine that in ten years from now customers are going to say: I really love Amazon, but I wish their prices were a little higher." — Jeff Bezos (cited Letter 22, June 2012)

The customer preference for low prices is not a trend or a cycle — it is a permanent feature of human nature. An insight of that durability is, in [[Information_as_Food]] terms, near-axiomatic: the highest possible shelf life. See also Letter 21's articulation of two ways to build a company (Bezos Wired interview): the high-margin model (Colgate, Nike, Coca-Cola) vs. the low-margin/large-customer-base model (Wal-Mart, Costco, Amazon). Sleep's portfolio is "firmly in the second camp."
