# Portfolio Classification Framework (Franchises / Asset-Backed / Deep Value)

## Summary
[[Nick_Sleep]] categorises the [[Nomad_Investment_Partnership]]'s holdings into three buckets throughout the early letters: (1) **franchise businesses** — difficult to copy, durable competitive moats; (2) **asset-backed businesses** — value anchored in balance sheet assets; and (3) **deep value workouts** — temporarily distressed, often indebted businesses. This taxonomy is a practical tool for understanding where portfolio risk and return are coming from.

## Explanation

### The Three Categories

#### 1. Franchise Businesses ("Difficult to Copy")
Businesses with structural competitive advantages that are hard to replicate — strong brands, regulatory licences, network effects, or captive distribution. Examples across Letters 1–4:
- [[International_Speedway_Corporation]] (exclusive Nascar track operator)
- [[Matichon]] (established Thai newspaper brand)
- [[Costco_Wholesale]] (EDLP membership model with self-reinforcing loop)
- [[Weetabix_Limited]] (70-year UK cereal brand; largest-selling by 2003)
- TV stations, consumer brand names, casinos (unnamed)

These businesses, Sleep notes, contain Nomad's **"current winners"** in Letter 2. They tend to grow intrinsic value over time, making patience especially rewarding.

**Typical allocation: 37–41% of fund.**

#### 2. Asset-Backed Businesses
Businesses where a large portion of appraised value is anchored in tangible assets — property, hotels, cash on the balance sheet, or the liquidation value of physical infrastructure. Examples:
- Hong Kong and Shanghai Hotels (mentioned in Letter 2 portfolio summary)
- Kersaf (South African conglomerate)
- Hotels, casinos, and conglomerates generally

These are lower-risk positions where the asset floor limits downside, but upside may be more limited than franchises.

**Typical allocation: 27–31% of fund.**

#### 3. Deep Value Workouts
Businesses where profitability is temporarily depressed — often due to excessive debt, accounting restatements, management transitions, or legacy liabilities — and where the underlying economic earning power is substantially higher than current reported results. Cash flow is the key anchor. Examples:
- Xerox Corporation (accounting restatement, debt — Letter 3)
- [[Stagecoach_Group]] (over-leveraged acquisition hangover — Letter 3)
- Conseco (failed — filed Chapter 11, December 2002)

Sleep notes these contain Nomad's **"current losers"** in the early period. The risk is that the turnaround takes longer, or does not happen.

**Typical allocation: 17–22% of fund.**

### Evolution Over Time
The framework itself remains stable across Letters 1–4, but the allocations shift as Sleep deploys cash and adds positions. By December 2002, franchise exposure had risen to ~41% and deep value to ~22%, reflecting deliberate additions during peak investor pessimism. By June 2003, the aggregate portfolio had moved from being valued at **50 cents on the dollar** to approximately **65 cents on the dollar** — Sleep was actively recycling appreciated positions into new 50-cent opportunities. See [[Margin_of_Safety]].

**Note on classification:** Some positions, like [[Weetabix_Limited]], straddle the framework. Weetabix has clear franchise characteristics (brand moat, dominant market share) but its listing on Ofex creates an illiquidity discount more typical of asset-backed investments. Sleep's approach is to note the discount's *source* rather than forcing rigid categorisation.

### What the Framework Reveals
The framework is more than a label — it tells you how to value each bucket and what to expect:

| Category | Primary Valuation Basis | Key Risk | Key Upside Driver |
|---|---|---|---|
| Franchise | FCF yield, brand power | Paying too much | Compounding moat value |
| Asset-backed | Book value, replacement cost | Asset overstatement | Asset realisation or re-rating |
| Deep value | Normalised FCF | Permanent impairment | Operational fix, debt paydown |
