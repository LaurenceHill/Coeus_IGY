# Destination Analysis

## Summary
Destination analysis is [[Nick_Sleep]]'s term for evaluating an investment by asking not what a business is worth today but what it will become over many years — its ultimate destination. The concept became "consciously central" to Nomad's process after lessons learned from the two largest mistakes of the partnership's early years: [[Conseco]] (anchored on original purchase analysis) and [[Stagecoach_Group]] (sold too soon because the destination was not properly assessed). Letter 13 (December 2007) articulates this most explicitly and quantifies the combined benefit of applying it.

## Explanation

### The Core Problem: Static Views
Both the Conseco and Stagecoach mistakes shared the same root analytical error:

> "The analytical mistake in both cases was to have a static view of a firm formed at the time of purchase, which failed to evolve as the facts changed." — Letter 13, December 2007

In Conseco's case, the investment team anchored on the analysis done at purchase to justify continued holding — even as the facts (securitised losses in manufactured housing, impairment of insurance capital, A.M. Best downgrade) changed around them. In Stagecoach's case, the static view was the original purchase thesis (which required a value above 14p per share). When that target was reached (shares sold at ~90p), the holding was exited — without asking what the business would become over the next decade. By the time of Letter 13, Stagecoach traded above £2.50.

### The Opportunity Cost Calculus
Sleep applies probability-adjusted opportunity cost analysis to both mistakes:

| Mistake | Immediate loss | Opportunity cost | Total cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Conseco | ~US&#36;5m to investors | ~US&#36;10m (adjusted for subsequent Nomad performance) | ~US&#36;10m |
| Stagecoach | Zero (profitable exit) | ~US&#36;12m (left £1.60+ on the table) | ~US&#36;12m |

The Stagecoach opportunity cost exceeds the Conseco loss — yet the industry focuses only on avoidable purchases (losers) and not on premature sales of winners. This is the "biggest error an investor can make" — selling a [[Stagecoach_Group|Stagecoach]] or [[Amazon]] or Wal-Mart while it is still compounding. See [[Longevity_of_Compound]].

### The Return on Prior Mistakes — 2007
By applying the destination analysis framework learned from these mistakes, Nomad avoided two large losses in 2007 and captured a large gain:

> "What we learnt at Conseco may well have kept us out of the US banks last year, and what we learnt at Stagecoach has helped us continue to own Amazon." — Letter 13, December 2007

Sleep estimates the combined gain from these two learnings at approximately **US&#36;60m in 2007 alone** — directionally correct if imprecise. He describes this as "a return on prior year losses." If the lessons continue to compound, the gains in future years will far exceed any one-year figure.

### The Right Questions
Destination analysis "helps us ask better questions and get to a firm's DNA." Rather than asking "is this stock still cheap?" (which anchors on the original purchase price), the destination analyst asks:

- What does this business look like in five or ten years?
- What would prevent it from reaching that destination?
- If the business reaches its destination, does it matter what I paid within a reasonable range?

This is why Wal-Mart could have been purchased at 150× its IPO price and still earned a 10% annual return — the destination was so good that the starting price mattered less than conventional heuristics would suggest. See [[Longevity_of_Compound]].

### Inversion as a Tool
Sleep also introduces inversion as a technique to help see destination more clearly:
- A newspaper headline claiming one-third of the population wants something also reveals two-thirds don't
- Companies making an investment in, say, growth spending, can be understood by inverting: what would the business look like if they stopped doing this?

The Bezos quote from Amazon's annual report (Letter 11) applied precisely this logic: even though the short-term math always says raise prices, the destination analysis says the long-run value of the moat built through price-sharing far exceeds what the math captures short-term. See [[Scale_Economies_Shared]].

### Three Cognitive Traps That Obstruct Destination Analysis
Sleep identifies three cognitive errors that prevent investors from thinking about destinations:

1. **Denial** — reinventing reality because the truth is too painful; refusing to update the thesis as facts change
2. **Anchoring** — treating the original purchase analysis as permanently valid; Keynes: "better to be generally right than precisely wrong" — yet applying precise price targets mechanically is the opposite
3. **Drift** — small incremental changes in thinking building into a big mistake without the investor noticing

See [[Mistakes_Framework]] for the full treatment of these three errors.
