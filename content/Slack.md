# Slack (Optionality in Systems)

## Summary
"Slack" is [[Nick_Sleep]]'s term — drawn from Nassim Taleb — for deliberate underutilisation of capacity: time, capital, and attention held in reserve to respond to unexpected opportunities or crises. Introduced in Letter 15 (December 2008) as a counterpoint to the financial crisis's root cause (output-maximisation at all levels), it describes how [[Nomad_Investment_Partnership]] is structured to maintain slack, why Buffett's cash is worth more than cash, and why "busy incompetence" is the dominant failure mode of large institutions.

## Explanation

### Taleb's Diagnosis
> "Capitalism does not teach slack, it teaches optimisation." — Nassim Taleb (cited Letter 15, December 2008)

Capitalism teaches that assets must be worked hard, outputs maximised, returns as high as possible. Sleep identifies this mindset everywhere: overfilled itineraries, children's days packed with activities, borrowing capacity fully utilised (or risk an "inefficient capital structure"), Blackberries on holiday, cash "idly slouching on deposit" treated as sinful.

> "When someone says he is busy, he means that he is incompetent... having a stupidly busy schedule isn't a sign of being important. It means that you have become insulated from the world." — Nassim Taleb (cited Letter 15)

Sleep's application: if you are too busy to think, "which part of the brain is making the decisions? Not the rational outer cortex, we suspect." The output-maximisation mindset is short-term efficient but not long-term optimal — it destroys the capacity for genuine thinking that prevents drift into major mistakes.

### Slack in Investing: Cash as Optionality
The financial equivalent of slack is holding cash when opportunity sets are not compelling — rather than deploying capital immediately at whatever yield is available.

Sleep uses the example of a plan sponsor who, instead of accepting an 8% return from a promoter (600bps above deposit rates), chooses to hold cash at 2% but mentally accounts for the future opportunity set at 12%. His "blended return" of 10% looks irrational to peers (auditors and lawyers would not endorse it), but reflects the real value of optionality:

> "In the hands of Warren Buffett, one could rationally argue that cash is worth more than cash." — Letter 15, December 2008

Buffett's answer when asked how to get rich: "Wait for an opportunity and invest the spare million dollars you have lying around." Slack cash is worth more than cash — but *only* if it is subsequently invested intelligently when the opportunity arrives. This is not an argument for hoarding cash generally; it is an argument for maintaining a "cash buffer, just in case, a little slack in the system."

### Nomad's Structural Slack
Sleep describes several ways Nomad deliberately runs with slack:

1. **Organisational**: "Zak is perfectly capable of running Nomad on his own." This means the partnership runs at something less than 50% capacity utilisation on a normal day — and can "gear up" when opportunities arise, as they did in the 2008 crisis.

2. **Portfolio**: Few investments held for long periods = low transactional and monitoring load. This creates intellectual space for "gentle contemplation, to retreat and simmer a little" — the second step of Gell-Mann's creative thinking process (Letter 12).

3. **Capital**: The refundable performance fee mechanism (the Contingent Performance Reserve) means fees are not paid out immediately but held in reserve — another form of structural slack that aligns Nomad's incentives with the long-term horizon.

### The Drift Risk from Lack of Slack
The most dangerous aspect of output-maximisation is not any single bad decision, but the drift it enables:

> "The biggest mistakes are the ones that did not look like mistakes at the beginning, after all bank robbers don't start out robbing banks, they pinch sweets, get away with it, and drift their way up." — Letter 15, December 2008

The 2008 financial crisis was the end-state of a decade of small drifts: corporations using language like "negative growth" and "market turbulence" to avoid honest description of failures; bank executives too busy marking the next loan to assess the destination of securitised mortgages; quarterly reporting and annual incentives reinforcing each other's short-termism.

> "The drift toward poor long-term outcomes is so much harder to spot if your reference periods are short, and one is too busy being busy to notice a lack of real world thinking." — Letter 15, December 2008

Contrast with Frederick "Henry" Royce, who made engineers sign the parts they made and correct faults in their own unpaid time — personal accountability and pride of craft, not output-maximisation. Royce built the world's finest cars; securitisation trusts built reputational ruins.

### Slack and the School Incentive Example
Sleep applies the slack concept to a school he governed in south-east London. The failing school had misaligned incentives: no incentive compensation for teachers, Friday afternoons off. The transformation came via a "desire to be better" incentive system: bonuses for attendance and results, house points for good work (more positive than negative), a five-day week.

The connection to investing: "What do incentives encourage?" Good incentives raise the probability of reaching a favourable destination. [[Scale_Economies_Shared]] creates incentives for customer reciprocation (the customer benefits, so the customer returns — a positive feedback loop). High-low retailing creates incentives for customer disloyalty (train the customer to buy on deal; volume becomes volatile; costs rise). Slack in a system means incentives can be designed for long-run results, not just immediate outputs.
