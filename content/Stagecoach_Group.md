# Stagecoach Group

## Summary
Stagecoach is the largest bus operating company in the UK and was [[Nomad_Investment_Partnership]]'s **largest single holding** as of December 2002 (~8.6% of fund). It is a classic [[Charlie_Munger]]-style "cancer surgery" turnaround: a genuine franchise business (UK bus operations) buried under a catastrophic debt-funded acquisition (Coach USA). Sleep purchased shares at 14p against an estimated intrinsic value of ~60p.

## Explanation

> **Coverage:** Fully detailed in Letter 3 (December 2002). Not revisited in Letters 4 or 6. Partial exit in Letter 5; full exit and error-of-omission conclusion in Letter 13.

### Business Overview
Stagecoach was deregulated into private hands in the early 1990s. Founder Brian Souter built a world-class low-cost UK bus operator by doing "simple things right" — collecting fares, running more buses at peak times, undercutting state competition, painting buses in distinctive colours. By 1998 the shares reached £2.85 (from a 20p IPO in 1993) and Souter was among the richest people in Britain.

### The Mistake: Coach USA
In the late 1990s Souter went into semi-retirement. Successor management, under pressure to grow, made a **debt-funded acquisition of Coach USA** — a leveraged roll-up of disparate US bus, taxi, and charter coach operations assembled by an LBO fund. The price was too high, the operations had little standalone merit, required heavy reinvestment, and absorbed management attention away from the UK core. The UK business, neglected, deteriorated.

Result: shares fell from £2.85 (1998) to **10p** (late 2002) — half the 1993 IPO price.

### The Turnaround Thesis
Souter returned from semi-retirement in mid-2002 after the dividend was cut and management were sacked. His plan:
1. Cease investment in weak US operations; sell worst US businesses at asset value.
2. Use high free cash flow from the modern UK bus fleet to repay debt.
3. Restore focus to the "jewel" — the UK bus business (which Souter called the "Cinderella business").

Banks were cooperative, even allowing the company to repurchase its own public bonds at sizeable discounts to face value (prioritising debt reduction over their own technical covenant interests).

### Valuation
> ⚠️ **Snapshot — Letter 3 (December 2002).** These figures are as of Nomad's initial purchase. Final exit at ~90p documented in Letter 13; see below.

| Metric | Value |
|---|---|
| Nomad purchase price | 14p |
| Current market price (early Jan 2003) | 33p |
| Sleep's estimated intrinsic value | ~60p |
| Dividend yield at Nomad cost | >12% |

Sleep's 60p intrinsic value was based on **modest debt repayment and zero UK growth** — deliberately conservative. All upside from UK bus growth or US asset sales was incremental.

### Key Mental Model
Sleep explicitly applies [[Charlie_Munger]]'s **"cancer surgery approach"**: cut the weak subsidiaries to reveal the franchise core. Coca-Cola's ten-fold rise in the 1980s after shedding non-core assets (shrimp farms, wineries, film studios, bottling plants) is cited as the canonical precedent.

## Updates from Later Letters

### Letter 5 (December 2003)
By December 2003, Stagecoach had appreciated significantly (the portfolio rose +79.6% that year). Sleep **partially sold** the position as part of the recycling discipline — capital was redeployed from appreciated holdings (valued at ~65 cents on the dollar portfolio-wide) into new positions still priced at ~50 cents on the dollar. See [[Margin_of_Safety]] for the recycling logic.

Sleep also used the Stagecoach outcome to make a point about cognitive biases: "The Partnership's investment in Stagecoach has in effect paid for the failure of [[Conseco]] six times over, and yet it was Conseco that we wrote to you about first." See [[Cognitive_Biases_in_Investing]].

The 60p intrinsic value thesis was confirmed. The Souter plan executed as intended.

### Letter 13 (December 2007) — Full Exit and Greatest Error of Omission
By Letter 13, Sleep acknowledged that the Stagecoach position had been fully exited at a high of approximately **90p** — a six-fold return on the 14p purchase price and well above the original 60p intrinsic estimate. But the shares had continued rising: by December 2007 they traded **above £2.50**.

> "Stagecoach was a success in the sense that shares purchased at 14p were sold at a high of around 90p. That is until one looks in the Financial Times to be reminded that the shares currently trade above £2.50. The mistake was to leave £1.60 on the table." — Letter 13, December 2007

The opportunity cost: approximately **US&#36;12m** at the time of writing. Sleep identified this as Nomad's **greatest error of omission** — larger in dollar terms than the Conseco loss. The root cause: a static view of the business formed at purchase (requiring only that the price exceed 14p) that failed to evolve as the destination became clearer. This is the direct origin of [[Destination_Analysis]] as a formal framework.

Sleep's closing observation: "What we learnt at Stagecoach has helped us continue to own Amazon." The Stagecoach and Conseco lessons combined are estimated to have generated approximately US&#36;60m of benefit during 2007 — keeping Nomad out of US banks and in Amazon.
