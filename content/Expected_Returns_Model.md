# Expected Returns Model

## Summary
The Expected Returns Model is [[Nick_Sleep]]'s explicit framework for what [[Nomad_Investment_Partnership]] is trying to achieve and why. Laid out in Letter 5 (December 2003) and reprinted as an appendix to Letter 6 (June 2004), it converts three simple assumptions — entry price, intrinsic value growth rate, and error rate — into a concrete long-run return target. It is one of the most important frameworks in the letters for understanding Sleep's investment process.

## Explanation

### The Base Case: Getting It Right

Sleep's starting assumptions when buying a share:
1. **Entry price: 50 cents on the dollar** of intrinsic value — see [[Margin_of_Safety]].
2. **Intrinsic value growth: ~10% per annum**, assuming management allocates capital consistently with value creation — see [[Owner_Oriented_Management]].
3. **Time horizon: five years**.

The arithmetic:
- US&#36;1 of intrinsic value, compounding at 10% p.a. for five years, grows to **US&#36;1.62**.
- Businesses that compound value at this rate are typically rewarded with a fair market valuation (i.e., the market eventually prices them at ~US&#36;1.62).
- Nomad entered at **50 cents** on that original dollar.
- Return from 50 cents to US&#36;1.62 over five years = **approximately 26% per annum**.

> "Our aim is to make investments at prices we consider to be fifty cents on the dollar of what a typical firm is worth. Capital allocation by investee companies must be consistent with value creation and, if this is the case, we expect that the real value of the business could grow at around 10% per annum…This happy outcome would imply a return from purchase price (50 cents) of around 26% per annum." — Letter 5, December 2003

### When Wrong: The Error Scenario

Sleep's most common mistake is misjudging **capital allocation decisions** at investee companies — firms that articulate a share repurchase or debt repayment strategy and then make acquisitions instead. [[Diworsification]] in action. Examples: Readers Digest (explicitly named in Letter 5).

The key observation: these capital allocation mistakes typically prevent *compounding* of value, but have "to date rarely resulted in a permanent decline in the share price to below our purchase price (50 cents)." Mistakes tend to **atrophy** (stay cheap and go nowhere) rather than collapse — with one acknowledged exception (Conseco, see [[Conseco]]).

### The Portfolio-Level Arithmetic

If half of Partnership assets are mistakes (flat over five years) and the other half compound at 26% p.a.:

| Component | Assumption | 5-year outcome |
|---|---|---|
| Winners (50% of portfolio) | 26% p.a. compounded | ~3.2× |
| Mistakes (50% of portfolio) | Flat | 1.0× |
| **Portfolio** | **Blended** | **~13% p.a. compounded** |

> "If over five years our mistakes are on aggregate flat, and our mistakes total half of Partnership assets, then this implies a compounded annual return of 13% for the portfolio as a whole." — Letter 5, December 2003

Sleep immediately cautions: "This model is daftly precise, a little too neat and the one thing we can almost guarantee is that returns will not be exactly 13%." The point is not precision — it is to give Partners a *sensible* frame of reference, counteracting any temptation to extrapolate the extraordinary gains of 2002–2003.

### The Two Prime Determinants

> "The prime determinants of outcome are price (sticking to 50 cents on the dollar) and capital allocation by management. The first is in our control…The second involves a subjective judgment about the quality of management, and an assessment about the sustainability of business returns in the long run. It is these factors that occupy almost all our time." — Letter 5, December 2003

This is Sleep's clearest statement of where his analytical effort is directed: not forecasting macroeconomics or earnings, but (1) maintaining price discipline and (2) assessing management character and capital allocation quality.

### Relationship to Other Concepts

- The 50-cents discipline → [[Margin_of_Safety]]
- The 10% intrinsic value growth assumption → [[Franchise_Businesses]] and [[Owner_Oriented_Management]] (businesses that can actually grow value at 10% over the long run)
- The "mistakes atrophy not collapse" observation → [[Portfolio_Classification_Framework]] (deep value workouts absorb capital allocation mistakes more readily than franchises)
- The 13% target → [[Patience_as_Competitive_Advantage]] (this is a five-year number; any one year can look very different)
- The framework itself → [[Terminal_Portfolio]] (the endgame: businesses that compound at 10%+ indefinitely without Sleep needing to sell and redeploy)

## Contradictions / Notes
- ⚠️ Sleep cautions repeatedly that 13% p.a. is a *model* output, not a guarantee or target. He specifically warns against mental extrapolation of the +79.6% result in 2003.
- The model's assumptions (10% value growth, 50% error rate) are stated as illustrative, not empirical. Sleep does not claim to track whether these assumptions are validated ex-post.
