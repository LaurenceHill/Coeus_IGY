# Amazon

## Summary
Amazon.com became one of the dominant holdings in [[Nomad_Investment_Partnership]] by Letters 11–12 (2006–2007), growing to approximately **one sixth of the portfolio** after a doubling in share price. [[Nick_Sleep]] uses Amazon to illustrate the [[Scale_Economies_Shared]] model in its most explicit form — Jeff Bezos's own language about "relentlessly returning efficiency improvements and scale economies to customers" provided the clearest statement of the investment thesis. Amazon also serves as the anchor case for [[Scaling_Laws]] (internet vs. high street skeletal structure) and the [[Equity_Yield_Curve]] (long-term judgment-based bets vs. short-term math).

## Explanation

### Investment Thesis — Scale Economies Shared
The [[Scale_Economies_Shared]] model is deployed by companies "which have now come to dominate Nomad: Costco, Dell, Amazon and Berkshire (GEICO, Nebraska Furniture Mart)" (Letter 11). Amazon is the clearest example of a management team that has articulated, in its own annual report, the logic of this model:

> "As our shareholders know, we have made a decision to continuously and significantly lower prices for customers year after year as our efficiency and scale make it possible... Our judgment is that relentlessly returning efficiency improvements and scale economies to customers in the form of lower prices creates a virtuous cycle that leads over the long-term to a much larger dollar amount of free cash flow, and thereby to a much more valuable Amazon.com." — Jeff Bezos, 2005 Amazon Annual Report (quoted Letter 11)

Bezos explicitly acknowledges that this decision cannot be made on pure math: price reductions reduce short-term free cash flow; the long-term benefit is a judgment call. Sleep's response: "I think Bezos would run a good investment fund: but that is the point, good investing and good business decisions are synonymous."

### The US&#36;26 Valuation Case — Summer 2006
> ⚠️ **Snapshot — Letter 11 (December 2006).** These figures reflect Amazon's position as of summer 2006.

Sleep's analysis of the implied message in Amazon's summer 2006 share price:

| Item | Value |
|---|---|
| Reported free cash flow | ~US&#36;500m p.a. |
| Discretionary investment spend above maintenance | ~US&#36;500m (capex + R&D + shipping subsidy + advertising) |
| Implied return if all discretionary growth spending cancelled | ~US&#36;800m p.a. (after taxes) |
| Fair value as a "cash cow" at US&#36;800m return | ~US&#36;10bn enterprise value |
| Implied share price (cash cow scenario) | ~US&#36;26 |

The market in summer 2006 was pricing Amazon as if management's growth spending had no value — as if the rational move was to cancel all discretionary investment and pay out cash. For a business growing revenues in excess of 20% per annum, Sleep considered this "an odd statement."

> "Investors are saying to Amazon management 'your growth spending has no value, you may as well turn yourself into a cash cow'!" — Letter 11, December 2006

The controversy is captured in Bezos's own words: "As our shareholders know..." — but judging by share volumes, they don't.

### Math vs. Judgment (Bezos on Decision-Making)
Bezos articulates a principle Sleep finds deeply resonant:

> "Math-based decisions command wide agreement, whereas judgment-based decisions are rightly debated and often controversial, at least until put into practice and demonstrated. Any institution unwilling to endure controversy must limit itself to decisions of the first type. In our view, doing so would not only limit controversy – it would also significantly limit innovation and long-term value creation." — Jeff Bezos, 2005 Amazon Annual Report (quoted Letter 11)

This maps directly to Sleep's distinction between short-term traders (who need math-provable theses) and long-term investors (who can absorb the uncertainty of judgment-based bets). The short-term investors keep selling; the long-term investor waits for the judgment to be proved correct.

### Skeletal Structure — [[Scaling_Laws]]
In Letter 12, Sleep applies Geoff West's [[Scaling_Laws]] framework to justify Amazon's growth potential:

- **High street retail**: complex skeleton; quality is patchy; most expensive end of distribution chain; many variable elements at the store level
- **Amazon's internet model**: simple skeleton; warehouse-to-customer direct; website quality is uniform everywhere; service elements (delivery speed, web functionality, range) are inherently more controllable and fixed-cost

> "Whilst quality is inherently patchy at most high street retailers, it is fixed at Amazon." — Letter 12, June 2007

The power law for internet retail is "very high" — it can scale faster and more profitably than traditional retail without skeletal breakdown. E-commerce was 2.7–3.1% of US retail in 2006 (US Census Bureau data). Sleep poses the question: what will it be in ten years?

### Portfolio Weight and the Inverted Construction Problem
> ⚠️ **Snapshot — Letter 12 (June 2007).** Portfolio sizing data reflects Nomad's state at this date.

By June 2007, Amazon had grown to approximately **one sixth of the Partnership** after a doubling in share price. Sleep acknowledges the anchoring error:

> "In allowing past habits to anchor portfolio construction we have probably made the mistake of a starting holding that was almost certainly too low." — Letter 12, June 2007

The conventional portfolio construction method (build from 1% → 2% → 3%) anchors positions at small numbers. Sleep poses the alternative: start from 100% and ask "how much should I reduce this to?" This inversion would produce a completely different (more concentrated) portfolio.

The resulting ~16% position creates short-term volatility, which Sleep explicitly dismisses as irrelevant: "The volatility does not bother Zak and me one jot."

### The Wal-Mart Analogy and Opportunity Cost
Sleep raises the most uncomfortable question about Amazon:

> "Would selling Amazon today be the equivalent mistake of selling Wal-Mart in 1980 (a similar time period after both companies' IPOs)?" — Letter 12, June 2007

This echoes Sleep's ongoing argument that the **biggest mistake an investor can make is selling a winner too early** — not buying a stock that goes bankrupt. Opportunity cost goes unrecorded in performance records, which is why the industry systematically under-recognises this error.

The parallel is direct: Sleep's stated greatest error is the sale of [[Stagecoach_Group]] (which has risen ever since it was sold), not the purchase of [[Conseco]]. Amazon is the live test of whether the same mistake will be repeated.

### Founder Orientation
Jeff Bezos is explicitly cited as a founder-managed business in the context of Letter 12's observation that over 85% of Nomad's portfolio is in founder or first-generation managed businesses. The "healthy disdain for Wall Street" that Bezos demonstrates — refusing to optimise for quarterly earnings at the expense of long-term compounding — is precisely the quality Sleep and Zakaria look for. See [[Owner_Oriented_Management]].

### Crisis Performance — Letters 15–16 (December 2008 / June 2009)
> ⚠️ **Snapshot — Letters 15–16.** Data below reflects Amazon's position in late 2008 / early 2009.

While US retail sales fell approximately 10% in Christmas 2008, Amazon's orders rose +16%. Trailing twelve-month revenues were up +60% since mid-2007. Sleep's characterisation: Amazon was "not participating in the recession."

By Letter 16, Sleep explicitly frames Amazon as Nomad's largest single position — approximately **30% of the Partnership** — the direct consequence of the inverted portfolio construction logic from Letter 12 (start from 100%; reduce from there). He quotes Sam Walton's response when asked about risks to his fortune: "That's easy — one of them is that I've only got one stock."

Sleep's own observation: "I've only got one stock" is precisely the risk that orthodox diversification theory is designed to eliminate — but it is also precisely the posture of the world's greatest compounders. See [[Diworsification]].

### Amazon as the Wal-Mart Question — Letter 16
Sleep revisits the Wal-Mart analogy explicitly in Letter 16:

> "We are not saying, for example, that Amazon is the next Wal-Mart. Time will tell on this front. But we are asking the question, what if?" — Letter 16, June 2009

If Amazon's structural advantages (internet skeletal structure + scale economies shared + founder orientation + cultural disdain for advertising) are as durable as Wal-Mart's were in 1972, then Amazon's current price will look cheap in retrospect for decades. The alternative — selling Amazon now — is what Sleep called "the equivalent mistake of selling Wal-Mart in 1980." See [[Longevity_of_Compound]].

### Investment Spending as Moat Construction — Letter 19 (December 2010)
Letter 19 provides the most detailed quantification of Amazon's deliberate investment programme and its relationship to moat-widening:

> ⚠️ **Snapshot — Letter 19 (December 2010).** Figures below reflect Amazon's position at that date.

Over the three years preceding Letter 19, Amazon's investment spending scaled dramatically:

| Item | Growth (3 years to end-2010) |
|---|---|
| Marketing spend | +3× |
| Capital investment (infrastructure) | +4×+ |
| Revenues | +2.5× |
| Shipping cost subsidy | +3× |
| Price giveback investment | ~US&#36;700m–US&#36;3.4bn per year |
| Total investment spend | US&#36;4.8–US&#36;7.5bn p.a. (14–21% of revenues) |

Sleep notes that price giveback — Amazon choosing to lower revenues relative to what traffic would bear — "can dwarf other items of investment spending but, by its nature, is excluded from conventional accounting formats." This is the key reason conventional financial analysis undervalues the moat being built: the most important form of investment is, by definition, invisible to the income statement.

Most strikingly: **Amazon's operating costs as a percentage of revenues are lower than Wal-Mart's** — a firm sixteen times Amazon's size at the time. Sleep calls this "an awesome combination": a capital cost advantage *and* an operating cost advantage simultaneously, scaling in the firm's favour as it grows.

Bezos (Wired magazine, cited Letter 21) makes explicit why Amazon chooses this path:

> "There are two ways to build a successful company. One is to work very, very hard to convince customers to pay high margins… The other is to work very, very hard to be able to offer customers low margins… We're firmly in the second camp. It's difficult – you have to eliminate defects and be very efficient. But it's also a point of view. We'd rather have a very large customer base and low margins than a small customer base and higher margins." — Jeff Bezos, Wired magazine (cited Letter 21, December 2011)

Sleep's gloss: by sharing efficiency gains with customers, Bezos "turns size, frequently an anchor on business performance, into an asset. In other words, the moat surrounding the firm deepens as the firm grows."

### Internet Retail as "Large but Young" — Letter 20 (June 2011)
Letter 20 adds a critical reframe of standard valuation assumptions for internet retailers. Conventional models assume that as companies grow large, their growth rate slows (regression to the mean). Sleep's argument: this assumption may be wrong for internet retailers, because their growth rate is constrained not by physical capacity but by the **rate at which consumer habits change**:

> "Our hunch is that the growth rate in internet retailing is regulated, not by physical capacity... but more by the rate at which our own incumbent habits and associations are replaced with more rational behaviour." — Letter 20, June 2011

The habit-change sequence is slow and sequential: books online first → comfortable → then trainers → subscriptions → groceries. "The process is more of a drift than epiphany." This means that even a very large internet retailer may be capturing only a small fraction of its eventual market — because the remaining market share sits behind habits that have not yet changed. Amazon may therefore be simultaneously **large** (by conventional revenue metrics) and **young** (relative to its addressable opportunity). Applying mean-reversion to such a company produces a valuation error of potentially factor-two or more over a two-year period.

### Bezos on Advertising — Letter 16
Sleep quotes Bezos directly: "Advertising is the price you pay for having an unremarkable product or service." Amazon does not advertise. This is the cleanest possible statement of the SES proposition: the price advantage *is* the marketing. When the customer proposition is genuinely superior, the customer returns without being bribed.

Sleep notes that Amazon could potentially quadruple its US revenues from existing product categories alone — before considering international expansion or new categories. The revenue trajectory from 2007 to 2009 (+60% in trailing twelve months) was already evidence that the platform was scaling faster than the market expected.
