# Air Asia

## Summary
Air Asia is Asia's largest low-cost airline and, by [[Nick_Sleep]]'s analysis, "probably the lowest cost airline in the world" at the time of Letter 14 (June 2008). It is a [[Scale_Economies_Shared]] investment — sharing its structural cost advantages with customers through the lowest fares in the region — and was Nomad's largest foreign shareholder position by mid-2008. Sleep notes Air Asia as evidence that the SES model works across industries and geographies, not just in retail.

## Explanation

### Business Model and Cost Structure
Air Asia borrowed its model from Southwest Airlines: point-to-point network, online ticket sales, no reserved seating, one plane type (A320). The result is a cost structure significantly below regional competitors:

| Airline | Cost per seat per kilometre (including fuel, Dec 2007) |
|---|---|
| Air Asia | ~3 US cents |
| Ryanair | ~4.5 US cents |
| Southwest | ~5.5 US cents |
| Malaysian Airlines (flag carrier) | ~4.5 US cents |

Air Asia's costs are below Malaysian Airlines' by one-third — and average revenue per seat kilometre at Air Asia is **below the cost per seat kilometre at Malaysian Airlines.** An Air Asia ticket from Kuala Lumpur to Bangkok (730 miles) was available for M&#36;75 (~US&#36;22) — US&#36;8 below Malaysian Airlines' discounted fare and US&#36;120 below the normal fare.

Sleep also notes Air Asia borrowed from Ryanair's ancillary model: cabin staff selling incentives, ancillary products, charging for complexity (multiple bags). Even with these additions, the effective seat pricing remains below competitors' cost bases.

### Scale Economies Shared Credentials
Air Asia is explicitly classified as a [[Scale_Economies_Shared]] business alongside Amazon, Costco, Carpetright, GEICO, and Nebraska Furniture Mart — representing ~45% of the Nomad portfolio by Letter 14. The mechanism:

- Structural cost advantage → shared with customers as lower fares
- Customers reciprocate ("the planes run full")
- Scale increases → further cost improvements possible
- Competitors face pricing pressure that leaves them sub-economic

By Letter 15 (December 2008), amid a recession, the SES model was validating in real time: Air Asia's revenues per seat per kilometre **rose 33%** while costs per seat per kilometre (excluding fuel) **fell 10%** — the opposite of what was happening to conventional airlines.

### Investment Thesis Complications
Sleep is characteristically candid about the challenges:

- Funding for 175 ordered A320s (one-third the size of Southwest after 40 years) is "not entirely secured, and lenders are twitchy"
- Oil at US&#36;140/barrel (at time of Letter 14) creates near-term margin pressure
- Malaysian Airline Systems, previously the slow-moving flag carrier, is resurgent and leading a price war
- "Perfectly reasonable people could come to different conclusions as to the firm's investment merits"

Despite these complications, Sleep believes the business will "win in the marketplace in the end" because its cost advantage forces competitors to operate at sub-economic returns. Ultimately, even at the discounted mid-2008 prices, the market was pricing Air Asia "at a meaningful discount to the value of the company owned fleet of planes" — implying the market believes the business should not exist. Sleep finds this conclusion absurd:

> "As such a valuation would imply that Southeast Asians, who are some of the most price conscious people on the planet, don't want cheap airfares!" — Letter 14, June 2008

### Portfolio Position
- Nomad is the **largest foreign shareholder** in Air Asia (Letter 14)
- Investment is described as leveraged to Air Asia's success — "outcomes are leveraged"
- Sleep estimates Air Asia has the potential to "earn Nomad investors 20% of NAV in years to come" (Letter 14), contrasted with the 1.5% NAV cost of the MBIA mistake

> ⚠️ **Snapshot — Letter 15 (December 2008).** Position size and metrics above reflect the portfolio at this date. See updates from Letters 18, 19, and 21 below.

### The Favourable Branches Argument — Letter 18 (June 2010)
Sleep uses Air Asia as the primary worked example of what he calls the "favourable branches" framework — the idea that for certain businesses, the odds of success at each subsequent stage improve as a result of what was achieved at the prior stage. Unlike conventional decision-tree analysis (which assigns fixed probabilities to branches and aggregates them), this argument is that some businesses have *improving* odds as they progress:

> "Take Air Asia: The firm was born with a no frills, cost culture with the result that, we estimate, it is the lowest cost airline in the world: this is favourable branch one. Favourable branch two: the employees take pride in the firm, suggest their own savings and the savings are implemented. Branch three: the savings exceed the peer group and are given back to customers in the form of lower prices. Branch four: the customer reciprocates and revenues rise. Branch five: further scale advantages lead to more savings per seat flown. Branch six: further customer reciprocation. Branch seven: the network builds and crowds out other, less efficient airlines. Branch eight: competitors go out of business?" — Letter 18, June 2010

Sleep's point: "The odds associated with any of these branches are not static but, in a hugely important way, they improve as one travels from branch to branch." Conventional equity valuation prices a business as an average of all possible futures — but Air Asia is not travelling to all branches simultaneously. It has already navigated branches one through four, which means branches five through eight are materially more probable than the aggregate valuation implies. Short-term investors are indifferent to this distinction. Long-term investors are not.

### Letter 19 Update (December 2010) — Continued Investment Through Recession
While most businesses cut back during 2009–2010, Nomad's firms continued investing. Air Asia was the exception only in the narrowest sense:

> "Even at Air Asia, our only sizeable firm to push out its investment plans at all, the delayed delivery of six planes in an order book of one hundred is hardly an indication of real retrenchment." — Letter 19, December 2010

The order book of 100 aircraft — against which a deferral of six was Sleep's only complaint — underscores the ambition of Air Asia's expansion programme relative to peers who were actively grounding fleets.

### Letter 21 Update (December 2011) — SES Validation and Founder Character
By Letter 21, Air Asia is cited directly in Jeff Bezos's Wired magazine interview as one of the companies exemplifying the low-margin/large-customer-base model: "Wal-Mart, Costco, AirAsia, Amazon, Asos." This is the clearest external validation that the SES framework Sleep had been applying since Letters 14–15 was being recognised as a coherent category.

Sleep also recounts a direct exchange with the founder of Air Asia (Tony Fernandes) on the subject of how the firm would respond to a catastrophic operational failure:

> "When we asked the founder of AirAsia what he would do if his firm put a plane in a swamp (!), he raised both hands in a gesture of having nothing to hide. That response strikes us as very healthy." — Letter 21, December 2011

Sleep's framing is that the real risk to a franchise business is not the operational incident itself but a disingenuous response to it — which destroys the bond of trust that is the underlying asset. The founder's instinctive transparency is therefore diagnostic of character and moat durability. See [[Character_as_Moat]].

At the time of Letter 21, the weighted average revenue growth of Nomad's firms (Air Asia included) was over 30% per annum — "organic growth with, if anything, falling prices and no acquisitions in a time of austerity and little economic growth."
