# Deep Discount to Replacement Cost

## Summary
Deep Discount to Replacement Cost is one of [[Nick_Sleep]]'s two primary investment models (the other being [[Scale_Economies_Shared]]). Together these two models account for approximately 45% of [[Nomad_Investment_Partnership]]'s assets by Letter 8 (June 2005). The model identifies businesses whose market capitalisation is drastically below the cost of physically replicating their assets, in situations where the economics will eventually force prices back to replacement cost — provided the business is not destroyed by external events in the interim.

## Explanation

### The Core Logic
Physical assets — cement plants, factories, infrastructure — cost real money to build. When a market sells a company at a fraction of that cost, it is implicitly assuming either that the assets will never earn a return, or that the return will not justify replacement. In most cases this assumption proves wrong: either the assets earn their way back to market value, or a strategic acquirer buys the business recognising the gap.

The model was first applied at scale during the **1997 Asian financial crisis**, when Sleep found businesses across the region priced at small fractions of replacement cost:
- **Siam Cement**: rose twenty-fold from the 1997 trough in eight years.
- **Pretoria Portland Cement (South Africa)**: bought at ~US&#36;20/ton of capacity in 1998; valued at ~US&#36;180/ton by Letter 8.

### Conditions Required
Sleep articulates the conditions for the model to work:
1. **Discretionary capital must not be invested to exacerbate the situation** — management must direct free cash flow to debt repayment, not expansion.
2. **Supply side must remain muted** — industry capex is zero (no new capacity being built).
3. **The business must not be nationalised or confiscated**.
4. **The business must have latent pricing power** — prices will eventually need to rise to justify asset replacement.

### Pricing Power as the Anchor
The critical phrase is "deep discount to replacement cost **with latent pricing power**." The pricing power is what creates the self-healing mechanism: eventually, if the industry cannot price at or above replacement cost, no one builds new capacity, supply tightens, and prices must rise. The physical reality of asset economics reasserts itself.

### Zimbabwe as the Active Example (Letter 8)
By 2005, Sleep was making modest investments in Zimbabwe — described as a "second investment model" in letter 8.

> ⚠️ **Snapshot — Letter 8 (June 2005).** Zimbabwe investment details below reflect this date.

The investment case centred on **Zimcem** (Zimbabwe's largest cement producer, after the local Pretoria Portland Cement subsidiary):
- Replacement cost of assets: ~US&#36;70–100m
- Market price on Harare Stock Exchange: approximately **1/70th of replacement cost**
- No debt
- Awful current business conditions: general inflation exceeds cement price inflation, demand is low

Sleep's logic: "a wait of ten years for normalisation would still yield wonderful results." The business needs to replace its assets eventually, which will require prices that (1) fund the capex and (2) economically justify it. Either that, or Zimbabwe will have to go without cement.

**Currency approach**: Sleep addressed the distorted official exchange rate (Z&#36;9,100 to the dollar vs. street rate of ~Z&#36;17,000) by purchasing Old Mutual shares in Johannesburg, re-registering them in Harare, and selling there. The portfolio is valued using the exchange rate implied by the Old Mutual share price arbitrage — approximating the street rate — rather than the official rate (which would create a spurious 86% paper gain).

**Key risk**: Political confiscation or nationalisation — which "does not have a zero probability." As a result, total Zimbabwe investments are kept modest (unlikely to exceed a few percent of the Partnership at cost even if Sleep could acquire all desired shares).

### Relationship to Union Cement (Letter 6)
This is the same model that led to the [[Union_Cement]] investment in the Philippines — bought at ~25% of replacement cost, ultimately acquired by Holcim at ~5× Sleep's purchase price. The frustration there was illiquidity preventing Sleep from buying more than 1% of fund assets. Zimbabwe carried an even more extreme discount but with political risk replacing illiquidity as the main constraint on position size.

### Zimbabwe: The Full Story and Exit — Letter 25
Letter 25 (December 2013) provides the complete retrospective on the Zimbabwe investment. In the mid-2000s, when economic conditions were "almost uniformly favourable around the world," Zimbabwe was a glaring anomaly: industrial assets, often monopoly or near-monopoly operations, were available for much less than their physical cost. Sleep personally crossed the border from South Africa to investigate.

**Three sectors chosen** to diversify nationalisation risk and invest alongside powerful major shareholders: cement, brewery, and construction/engineering.

**The exchange control workaround**: Zimbabwe's exchange controls artificially overvalued the Zimbabwean dollar. Sleep's solution — used in Letter 8 and confirmed in Letter 25 — was to buy shares in firms listed on the Johannesburg Stock Exchange that also had a Harare listing, re-register those shares in Harare, and then sell on the Harare exchange. The uplift in purchasing power was "many fold above the rate offered by the central bank."

**What followed** (summarised in Letter 25):
- Economic contraction of up to ~50%
- Hyperinflation and abandonment of the Zimbabwean dollar as a medium of exchange
- Suspension of the stock exchange (zero value attributed to holdings during suspension)
- Violent, forced nationalisation of privately owned farms
- Attempted assassination of political opponents
- Coalition government including incumbent President Mugabe

**Outcome**: Despite all of the above, Nomad's Zimbabwean investments ultimately rose **3–8× in US dollar terms**. The final investments were sold the year before Letter 25 (2012/2013), with proceeds remitted to Nomad's London bank accounts. Sleep: "It is quite a story but, not one we would repeat in a hurry (we can do better with the compounding businesses these days — and they are much less stressful)."

**The souvenir**: Each limited partner received a **100 trillion Zimbabwean dollar banknote** — issued at the peak of hyperinflation, today worthless — as a memento of Nomad's "adventures in capitalism."

### Comparison to Scale Economies Shared
| Dimension | Scale Economies Shared | Deep Discount to Replacement Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Primary example | [[Costco_Wholesale]] | Zimcem, Siam Cement, [[Union_Cement]] |
| Source of return | Franchise growth + rerating | Asset rerating to replacement cost |
| Time horizon | Indefinite (permanent holding possible) | Medium-term (until normalisation) |
| Key risk | Culture drift / management change | Political/confiscation; extended depression |
| Position sizing | Large (Kelly-justified concentration) | Small (constrained by liquidity and political risk) |
