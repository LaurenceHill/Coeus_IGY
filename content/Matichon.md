# Matichon

## Summary
Matichon is Thailand's second Thai-language newspaper. It was one of [[Nomad_Investment_Partnership]]'s earliest disclosed holdings (~3.2% of fund at December 2001) and exemplified Sleep's thesis of a franchise business in an emerging market, available at a cyclical trough valuation, with a family-run management team demonstrating excellent operational discipline.

## Explanation

### Business Description
Matichon is a Thai-language newspaper described as "tabloid in style" and editorially pro-reform — appealing to the post-Asian-crisis generation of Thais raised with higher living standards and more Western values than their parents. It is the second-largest Thai-language paper, behind Thai Rat. [[Nick_Sleep]] notes he could not read Thai but relied on Thai contacts for editorial assessment.

[[Marathon_Asset_Management]] was also a shareholder in Matichon — the third-largest — meaning Nomad and Marathon were co-investors with aligned views.

### Investment Thesis

#### Franchise Quality
- **Established brand** with a pro-reform editorial identity that differentiated it from "old Thailand" competitors.
- **Circulation growth accelerated** after the 1997 Asian financial crisis, indicating resilience and genuine reader loyalty rather than bubble-era growth.
- **Family management**: The controlling family **avoided** the classic pitfalls — no diversification into new media, no gambling on new titles. Capital was focused on building long-term readership and margins.

#### Cyclical Trough Valuation
Matichon's revenues were primarily advertising-driven (cover price kept low to build circulation), making it highly cyclical. The advertising cycle was at trough:
- Revenues had declined by one third peak to trough.
- Despite this, the firm's cost-cutting was exceptional: **free cash flow in 2000 was 40% higher than in 1996** on sales one-third lower.
- Advertising rates had recovered 20% from trough but were still ~50% below the previous peak — substantial upside remaining.

#### Valuation
> ⚠️ **Snapshot — Letter 1 (December 2001).** These figures are as of Nomad's initial purchase. Matichon briefly revisited in Letter 9 (hostile bid Sep 2004; Nomad held). No further substantive updates.

| Metric | Value |
|---|---|
| Shares peaked at | Tb300 (US&#36;12) in 1994 |
| Purchase price | Tb50 (US&#36;1) |
| EV / Revenue | 0.75x |
| EV / Normalised FCF | ~4x |
| Discount to intrinsic value | ~67–75% of estimated worth |
| Dividend yield at Nomad's cost | ~9% |

Sleep estimated Matichon was worth approximately three times its market price, or as cheap as one-quarter of the valuation of Western newspaper peers. The family had **tripled the dividend** since the trough — generating a 9% yield while shareholders waited for the advertising cycle to recover.

#### Balance Sheet Strength
The firm was **debt-free** — a noteworthy feature in post-Asian-crisis Thailand where many businesses were encumbered by foreign-currency debt.

### Risk
- Currency risk: the share price decline incorporated a large element of baht depreciation. Sleep adjusts for this explicitly.
- Advertising cycle timing: "improve slowly it is, rates are expected to rise gently this year and next, so patience is likely to be rewarded here too."
- Language/culture gap: Sleep is candid that he cannot read Thai and relies on third-party editorial assessment.

## Takeover Attempt — Letter 9 (December 2005)
In September 2004, Matichon was the subject of a hostile takeover attempt reportedly backed by the Thai Prime Minister, who had been regularly criticised in Matichon's pages. The bid was debt-financed and orchestrated through another local media business.

In the weeks before the attempt, other sizeable institutional shareholders approached Nomad and asked if it wished to sell its shares in a block to the bidding party. Sleep declined, finding the price too low. He documented the psychological mistakes the selling institutions appeared to be making simultaneously:
1. **Social proof**: peers wanted to sell → must be OK to sell
2. **Availability (vivid evidence)**: the stock had risen → it is OK to sell; shares are illiquid → we do not want to get stuck
3. **Principal-agent conflict**: a business reason existed to sell (liquidity pressure) independent of investment merit
4. **Poor probability-based thinking**: "highly priced shares are rarely illiquid!"

The result: most institutional holders sold. Nomad declined, alongside the company founder and reportedly the Thai PM's associates. Following the institutional exit, Matichon began a **sizeable share repurchase programme** — not good for liquidity, but strongly accretive to per-share value for remaining owners.

Sleep used this episode in Letter 9 to illustrate how institutional constraints and psychological biases combine to produce commercially expedient (not investment) decisions. "Time will tell whether these investment institutions were right to sell but, if they turn out to be, it will be for the wrong reason." See [[Probability_Based_Thinking]] and [[Principal_Agent_Conflict]].

The Matichon bid was one of **six Nomad investments that had received takeover approaches** in the eighteen months to December 2005 — approximately one quarter of the portfolio by count.
