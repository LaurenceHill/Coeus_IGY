# Union Cement

## Summary
Union Cement is the Philippines' largest cement company and one of [[Nomad_Investment_Partnership]]'s most frustrating investment experiences — not because it went wrong, but because Sleep could barely buy any. Priced at ~25% of asset replacement cost, Sleep spent six months as almost the only buyer, acquired just ~1% of fund assets (US&#36;1m), and watched Holcim subsequently acquire a control block at ~5× his purchase price. It is the canonical example in the letters of the **illiquidity trap in deep value**.

## Explanation

### The Investment Thesis
At the time of Nomad's investment, Union Cement's shares traded at approximately **one quarter of the replacement cost** of its assets. The assets were world-scale cement plants built during the 1990s Asian boom — modern, large, and expensive to replicate. The low market valuation had the predictable effect: management stopped spending on discretionary capex (why invest in assets the market values at 25 cents on the dollar?) and redirected free cash flow to debt repayment. The company was actively self-healing — but investors weren't paying attention.

Sleep notes the shares had already fallen from US&#36;0.30 and were bouncing between 1c and 2c per share.

### The Liquidity Paradox
Sleep started buying and — as almost the only buyer — **raised the price from 1.5c to 2.5c over six months**. Despite this, he managed to acquire only **US&#36;1m worth of stock** in a company with a US&#36;200m market cap and ~US&#36;700m in gross assets.

The implied holding period from share turnover during this period: **40+ years**. This is not irrational from the holders' perspective — Sleep observes it is "coincidentally comparable to the life of a cement plant." Long-duration assets owned by investors with extremely long horizons simply don't trade.

> "The more undervalued the company became the less the shares traded, as more holders understood the under-valuation." — Letter 6, June 2004

This is an important inversion of normal market logic: in most assets, rising prices attract more buyers. In deeply undervalued, illiquid assets, the more obvious the undervaluation becomes, the *fewer* willing sellers there are.

### The Outcome
Holcim, one of the world's largest cement companies, purchased a control block at an effective price of **~10c per share** — five times Sleep's average purchase price. Nomad's tiny 1% position delivered a ~5× return, but Sleep estimates he *could* have made this a 10% position. The profit was a fraction of what it should have been.

> "Charlie Munger is right when he says, 'it is aggravating to just buy a bit.'" — Letter 6, June 2004

### Lessons
1. **Illiquidity is both the source of the discount and the barrier to exploiting it.** The same characteristic that makes Union Cement cheap (thin trading, no institutional interest) prevents large capital deployment.
2. **Deep-discount assets in illiquid markets require very long time horizons and small position sizes.** Sleep could not force the market to sell him shares at 2c — he could only wait.
3. **The [[Kelly_Criterion]] is constrained by market reality.** Even at 75%+ conviction on Union Cement's thesis, the optimal Kelly position was unachievable. Sleep wanted 10%; the market gave him 1%.
4. **Fund size matters.** As Nomad grew, individual opportunities that would have been 5–10% positions at US&#36;10m AUM became 1–2% positions at US&#36;100m. This is one of Sleep's explicit reasons for closing the fund.

> ⚠️ **Snapshot — Letter 6 (June 2004).** Position exited when Holcim acquired control at ~10c. Position was ~1% of fund. Final return ~5× purchase price.
