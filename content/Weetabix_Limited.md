# Weetabix Limited

## Summary
Weetabix Limited is a UK breakfast cereal manufacturer (Weetabix, Alpen, Ready Brek, Weetos) and one of [[Nomad_Investment_Partnership]]'s significant holdings, first invested in early 2003. It is a privately-held franchise business with a near-unchanged share count since the 1930s, trading on the illiquid Ofex market at a ~70% discount to Sleep's estimate of intrinsic value. It is the investment that prompted Nomad to expand its mandate to include **unlisted equity**.

## Explanation

### Background
Weetabix was established by the **George family in 1932** as Britain's answer to imported breakfast cereals, primarily from Kellogg. Initial capital came from the family and local farmers (securing commodity supply). The firm has required **no further equity capital** since founding and its share count is essentially unchanged since the 1930s — some farmers have sold their shares, forming the free float that trades on **Ofex** (the UK equivalent of the US pink sheets), not the London Stock Exchange.

Chairman: **Sir Richard George**.

### Business Quality
Weetabix exemplifies the [[Franchise_Businesses|franchise business]] characteristics Sleep values most:

- **~22% share of the UK breakfast cereal market** — the largest-selling breakfast cereal in the UK by Letter 4 (2003), overtaking Corn Flakes 70 years after founding.
- **High advertising/marketing spend** — the Chairman describes this as "investment in the future." Weetabix's share of voice (share of industry marketing spend) **exceeds** its share of market — a powerful combination that typically leads to rising market share over time.
- **Proven brand resilience**: A major UK supermarket suspended Weetabix in favour of own-brand products. Own-brand sales disappointed; the supermarket resumed stocking Weetabix. This is a direct, real-world test of franchise durability.
- **50% spare manufacturing capacity** — deliberately maintained for reliability and delivery. Lowers near-term returns on capital but builds long-run reputation. Sleep: "Value creation is often most sustainable when it is built slowly."
- **No debt, cash-rich balance sheet** — including £5/share of cash and property on books at 1988 prices (significantly understated).
- **[[Per_Share_Discipline]]**: No share issuance since the 1930s.

### Why It Was Cheap
The discount had nothing to do with business quality. Three structural reasons:

1. **Ofex listing**: Large institutions cannot invest via Ofex under their mandates — a classic example of rules constraining capital from going where value lies. See Sleep's critique of narrow fund management mandates in Letter 4.
2. **Illiquidity**: Low free float, modest market cap, limited City analyst coverage.
3. **No forced sellers or buyers**: A business with no debt and no institutional pressure to transact.

> "Weetabix is just our sort of company." — Letter 4, June 2003

### Mandate Change
This investment required [[Nomad_Investment_Partnership]] to **change its investment policy** to include unlisted equity — approved unanimously by Partners in February 2003 after Sleep sought permission in January. The speed of the Partners' collective response allowed Nomad to invest while prices were "particularly depressed." Sleep credits the Partners explicitly.

### Valuation
> ⚠️ **Snapshot — Letter 4 (June 2003).** Position fully resolved — see Letter 5 exit section below.

| Metric | Value |
|---|---|
| Nomad purchase price | ~£20/share |
| Sleep's DCF estimate (modest FCF growth) | ~£70/share |
| Implied private market value (PepsiCo/Quaker Oats multiple) | ~£75/share |
| Cash on balance sheet | £5/share |
| Property (book value, 1988 prices) | Significantly understated |
| Profit vs. purchase price (as of Letter 4) | +20% |

The acquisition comparables Sleep uses: PepsiCo's price for Quaker Oats (ex-Gatorade) and the bid received by Hershey — both consumer brand transactions that imply a private market value of ~£75/share for Weetabix.

Sir Richard George mentioned, in a "rare indiscretion," that over 30 years he had rejected numerous acquisition offers from international food groups at prices consistent with these multiples.

### Broader Lesson: Mandate Breadth as Edge
Sleep uses Weetabix to argue that most fund managers could never have made this investment — their mandates restrict them to listed securities, specific geographies, or minimum market caps. Nomad's deliberately broad mandate is why the fund was named "Nomad" — it goes wherever value is.

> "There are more tools in Nomad's box than are available to most fund managers." — Letter 4, June 2003

### The Hicks Muse Takeover (Letter 5, December 2003)

> ⚠️ **Exit event — Letter 5 (December 2003).** Weetabix was acquired via a Scheme of Arrangement. Sleep exited involuntarily at a price he considered ~25% below intrinsic value.

At the end of 2003, US private equity firm Hicks, Muse, Tate and Furst bid **£53.75 per share** for Weetabix — a Scheme of Arrangement requiring only 75% shareholder approval (vs. 90% for a General Offer). The George family, who had signed irrevocable undertakings to accept the offer, owned enough shares to ensure the threshold was met.

Sleep's verdict: the price was **~25% below intrinsic value**. His reasons for concern:

1. The Scheme of Arrangement structure made it harder for minority shareholders to block the deal than a General Offer would.
2. The George family received employment contracts post-sale — a benefit not available to outside shareholders.
3. The highest bidder was a private equity firm (which intends to sell at a profit) rather than a strategic acquirer (which would benefit from duplicate-cost removal over decades). This suggested the auction had not been properly run.
4. The family's irrevocable undertakings would only be broken at >25% premium to the Hicks Muse offer — signalling the family knew it was cheap.

Sleep wrote to Sir Richard George, met the investment bankers, and **voted against the deal** — describing it as "a cry in the dark" since Nomad's holding was too small to influence the outcome. The Court required him to sell under the Scheme. Nomad realised a profit (~2.7× from ~£20 to £53.75), but Sleep argued ~25% of true value was left on the table.

**The broader lesson:** Sleep uses the Weetabix episode to indict the entire UK small-cap privatisation phenomenon — institutional shareholders had abandoned small-caps for mega-caps, leaving fragmented registers dominated by insider interests. Schemes of Arrangement then allowed the most ruthless assertion of one party's will over passive shareholders.

> "In no other sphere of capitalism can your property be seized in exchange for cents on the dollar (except compulsory purchase on the grounds of national interest)." — Letter 5, December 2003

The direct consequence: Sleep stated his intention that as Nomad grows in size, he will build positions large enough (>10%) to influence outcomes. See [[Active_Ownership]].
