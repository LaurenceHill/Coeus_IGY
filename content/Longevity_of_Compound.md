# Longevity of Compound

## Summary
"Longevity of compound" is [[Nick_Sleep]]'s phrase for the investment anomaly that truly exceptional businesses — those with durable structural advantages — remain chronically undervalued in the stock market for decades because investors apply average-company failure rates to above-average companies. The concept is most fully developed in Letter 16 (June 2009) via the Wal-Mart "cheap for decades" chart, but is implicit throughout the Nomad letters from the first analysis of [[Costco_Wholesale]] onwards. It is the logical foundation for [[Destination_Analysis]], [[Scale_Economies_Shared]], and the [[Terminal_Portfolio]] concept.

## Explanation

### The Core Anomaly
Investors know, on average, that good things do not last. Empirical Research Partners established that the probability of a growth stock maintaining growth-stock status for five years is **1 in 5**; for ten years, **1 in 10**. So the rational prior is: assume mean reversion quickly.

But this average is applied indiscriminately — even to the outlier companies whose structural advantage *prevents* mean reversion. The result:

> "The shares of great companies can therefore be cheap, in some cases, for decades." — Letter 16, June 2009

### The Wal-Mart Evidence
Sleep presents the most powerful illustration via a two-line chart:
- **Blue line**: Wal-Mart's actual share price since IPO (1972)
- **Red line**: The price an investor could have paid at any time and still earned 10% per annum through to 2009

> "If, in 1972, an investor chose to make a purchase... he could have paid over one hundred and fifty times the prevailing share price (a price-to-earnings ratio of over fifteen-hundred times) and he would have still earned a ten percent return on his investment through to today." — Letter 16, June 2009

The red line stayed above the blue line for most of the period — meaning Wal-Mart was *cheap relative to its destination* for the better part of four decades, despite appearing expensive by every conventional heuristic. An investor who waited until 10 years after IPO could still have paid 200× prevailing earnings and earned 10%.

### Why Investors Repeatedly Misread This

Sleep offers three categories of explanation for why no one but the founding Walton family owned Wal-Mart all the way through:

**1. Misanalysis — wrong mental model ("Barbie Problem")**
- Investors are conditioned by companies that have one good idea, then dilute returns (Mattel struggled to replicate the economics of Barbie — hence "Barbie syndrome")
- Applying this template to Wal-Mart misses the source of success entirely: the price-giveback strategy did not change from year to year
- **Culture** plays a central role in sustaining the strategy, but culture is hard to quantify and therefore goes undervalued
- Valuation heuristics are used rather than assessing real business value
- DCF models assume mean reversion in years 3 or 5 — by convention — not by analysis

**2. Structural/Behavioural**
- Active fund managers need to look active: selling a "expensive-looking" Wal-Mart to buy something "cheaper" is what activity looks like
- Investors are not long-term and do not look further than the next few years
- Career risk: espousing a 10-year view on a firm is a "hostage to fortune"
- Marketing departments require new stories: new stocks provide new stories
- Fund managers sell winners to appear diversified to clients

**3. Incorrectly Weighting the Odds**
- "Investors tend not to believe in 'longevity of compound'" — Sleep attributes this to his first boss
- Conventional thinking: good things do not last; average failure rates applied to outliers
- These failure rates are statistically correct on average, but the average is the wrong reference class for exceptional businesses

### The Weighting Problem
The core failure is not seeing the information — investors do see the quarterly results and cheer — but incorrectly weighting it:

> "Investors recognize success incrementally when the factors that lead to success, such as scale economics shared reinforced by a strong culture, may be *constant.*" — Letter 16, June 2009

If the strategic logic (scale economies shared + culture that celebrates thrift) is constant, then the insight available at IPO is not less valid than the insight available 20 years later. But investors price the stock as if the source of success is perpetually in question.

### IBM and Wal-Mart — The Industry's Greatest Omission
Sleep recounts a story about a "large successful fund management company" that discovered in the early 1970s that its 30-year-old sale of IBM had been a catastrophic error of omission — if they had held, the IBM stake alone would have been larger than total AUM. The firm agreed to learn from this mistake, went back to their desks — and then made the identical error: selling Wal-Mart, which 30 years later would again be worth more than their total AUM.

> "In terms of dollars of opportunity lost, it is likely to be the biggest single error this firm will make." — Letter 16, June 2009

This is the [[Destination_Analysis]] problem at scale: the firm saw the information, had the lesson, and still anchored on short-term heuristics ("appears expensive") rather than asking what the business would become.

### The Amazon Question
Sleep explicitly frames Amazon as the open test of this thesis in 2009:

> "We are not saying, for example, that Amazon is the next Wal-Mart. Time will tell on this front. But we are asking the question, what if?" — Letter 16, June 2009

And earlier in the letters: "Would selling Amazon today be the equivalent mistake of selling Wal-Mart in 1980?" — Letter 12. If the structural factors driving Amazon's advantage (internet skeletal structure + scale economies shared + founder orientation) are as durable as Wal-Mart's were in 1972, then the current price, whatever it is, will look cheap in retrospect.

### Internet Retailers: Large but Young — Letter 20 (June 2011)
Letter 20 adds a structural dimension specific to internet retailers. Standard valuation models assume growth decelerates as companies become large — mean reversion. Sleep argues this assumption fails when a company's growth is constrained not by physical capacity but by the pace of consumer habit change:

> "Our hunch is that the growth rate in internet retailing is regulated, not by physical capacity… but more by the rate at which our own incumbent habits and associations are replaced with more rational behaviour. The process is more of a drift than epiphany." — Letter 20, June 2011

The habit-change sequence from books to trainers to groceries is slow and sequential. A very large internet retailer may therefore still be capturing only a fraction of its eventual market — "although some online retailing firms may be quite large, they may also be quite young." Two years of 40% annual revenue growth would double revenues in 24 months. A regression-to-mean analyst would forecast something half that size. The valuation error compounds rapidly — and structurally reinforces the longevity-of-compound dynamic: the market applies mean-reversion logic to a business whose structural position prevents it.

### The Monopoly of Honest Long-Term Investing
The final implication of longevity of compound:

> "There is, albeit with a few notable exceptions, almost no competition for the long-term investor who has done his homework. Isn't it exciting that honest, simple, long-term investing is so, well, un-exciting." — Letter 16, June 2009

The competitive moat for the investor is precisely the same as the competitive moat for the business: *time horizon*. The average fund manager's career incentive structure demands results within 3–5 years. Anyone willing to extend that horizon to 10–20 years is operating with almost no competition. This is the investor analogue of the [[Equity_Yield_Curve]]: the long end is less competitive, not more.
